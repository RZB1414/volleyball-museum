# PARTE 1 — Gramatica de level design (Resident Evil)

## Principios centrais

- THE SPACE IS THE ANTAGONIST. Mikami's Spencer Mansion is explicitly designed as a security system rather than a residence — critics describe it as 'a machine for impeding us.' The building's job is to withhold, not to deliver. Before designing a single room, decide what each space refuses to give the player. Every architectural feature must be a gate, a promise, or a payoff; if it is none of those, it is decoration and should be cut or merged.
- RECURSIVE UNLOCKING — THE MAP OPENS LIKE A SPIRAL SHELL. The player starts with access to a handful of rooms. Keys found inside accessible space unlock doors in ALREADY-VISITED space, which contain the next key. The accessible footprint grows outward in loops rather than forward in a line. Data-visualization analysis of RE1 counted 116 unique rooms across four macro-areas, with 44 rooms (38%) visited only once on an optimal route and the single most-trafficked room (a small connector hub) hit 8 times — proof that the design deliberately localizes each puzzle next to its solution so non-linearity never degrades into wandering.
- SHOW THE LOCK LONG BEFORE THE KEY. Foreshadowing is the engine of desire. In RE2R the player meets spade-emblem doors on the ground floor of the RPD but does not receive the Spade Key until the third floor, having passed through the west office, armoury, darkroom and locker room in between. The gap between seeing and solving is where curiosity lives — but only because the map records the lock by name, so the gap is a mystery rather than amnesia. Reverse this order and you get a pocket full of unmatched keys and a brute-force search.
- COMPRESSION OVER SPRAWL. A physically small space is made to feel enormous by forcing the player to traverse it in many different configurations. RE1's mansion is 59 rooms and 43% of the runtime; RE7 spends 10-12 hours in essentially one house; RE2R's police station is a single building. The design goal is reuse of space, not quantity of space. Every new key should make the OLD map feel different, not just add new map.
- TENSION IS METERED, NOT CONSTANT — THE SAFE-ROOM CONTRACT. RE2R director Kazunori Kadoi: they judged that unbroken tension would be exhausting, so save rooms were designed as a place to breathe. The release valve is a contract: nothing bad ever happens here. The contract is the currency; break it once and every save room becomes tense forever after, permanently destroying the mechanic. Design the relief beats with the same rigour as the tension beats.
- KNOWLEDGE IS THE REAL PROGRESSION; KEYS ARE ONLY TOKENS. The player's mental model of the building is the actual upgrade being earned. Writing on RE7's Baker house notes that repeated returns converted fear into competence — familiarity with exits became a tactical asset. Reward the internalized map: the second traversal of a wing should be measurably faster and more confident than the first, and the game should notice.
- WITHHELD SIGHTLINES MANUFACTURE IMAGINATION. Fixed cameras (RE1/RE2), over-the-shoulder (RE4/RE2R/RE4R) and first-person (RE7/RE8) are three technologies for the same job: preventing the player from scouting ahead. Fixed angles frame the shot cinematically and direct attention while hiding what is off-frame; OTS deliberately crops the frame tight for claustrophobia; first-person makes the space intimate. Whichever you pick, the design requirement is identical — the player must never be able to fully audit a space from its threshold.
- EVERY SYSTEM IS DIEGETIC. The typewriter is the save. The item box is the inventory. The map is a paper map you found in the building. The door is a door. The fiction absorbs the mechanic so no UI abstraction ever punctures the place. Frictional Games' analysis of RE7 makes the point that ordinary puzzles become exciting when the fiction explains who put them there — the mansion's absurd weathervane and piano locks are forgiven precisely because the building is established as a paranoid security apparatus.
- LAYERED READERSHIP: A THIN CRITICAL PATH INSIDE A BROAD OPTIONAL LAYER. The same geometry supports a 4-hour rush and a 12-hour completionist run because files, treasures, examine-secrets, locked drawers and optional wings hang off a short spine. Speedrun analysis suggests the designers laid down the fastest path first and grew secondary objectives around that spine.
- LEGIBILITY OF STATE IS NON-NEGOTIABLE. At any moment the player must be able to answer three questions without leaving the fiction: where haven't I been, what did I leave behind, and what am I currently locked out of. RE2R answers all three on one screen — red rooms still hold items, blue rooms are cleared, left-behind items render as miniature icons, puzzles show as an exclamation mark with a tooltip naming the puzzle, and every locked door is auto-labelled with its lock type.
- ESCALATE BY ALTERING KNOWN SPACE, NOT BY ADDING NEW SPACE. REmake's Crimson Heads make cleared corridors dangerous again; RE7 repeatedly re-hazards the main house; RE2R's Mr X invades territory you had mastered. The most efficient escalation is making the second visit to a familiar room materially different from the first. Analysis of RE1 identifies a deliberate full-mansion re-traversal at roughly 60% completion that reestablishes familiar ground and then subverts it.
- SECTION THE GAME TO FIGHT FATIGUE. RE1 splits into mansion (43%), guardhouse and courtyard (32%) and laboratory (25%), each with its own palette, threat set and room-transit rhythm. RE8 pushes this furthest: one village hub feeding four tonally distinct lord domains — gothic castle, claustrophobic doll house, swamp, industrial factory. Variety of texture, not variety of verb, is what sustains a long exploration game.

## Gramatica espacial

- ONE LEGIBLE HUB WITH HIGH BRANCH COUNT. Spencer Mansion's main hall, the RPD Main Hall, RE8's Village. The hub is multi-storey with visible verticality, contains a strong central landmark (grand staircase, the Goddess Statue), and exposes 5-8 doors of which most are initially locked. It is the room the player is forced through most often — RE1's most-visited room is a small connector at the junction of several shortcut paths, hit 8 times in an optimal run. The hub must be instantly recognizable from any entrance and from any floor.
- SPOKES ARE SHORT, THEMED WINGS THAT TERMINATE IN A KEY. A wing is 4-10 rooms with its own visual identity and its own micro-narrative, and it ends by handing back the credential that reopens the hub. Never chain two wings end-to-end without a return to the hub; the return trip is what teaches the shape of the building.
- EVERY WING MUST CLOSE WITH A ONE-WAY SHORTCUT BACK TO THE HUB. A ladder that drops down, a shutter you raise from inside, an elevator you power on, a crate you shove aside. The shortcut is the actual reward of the wing and it retroactively shrinks the wing to a corridor. Analysis of the mansion notes explicitly that as doors open, shortcuts appear and backtracking becomes rewarding because the player learns quicker, safer routes.
- THE ONE-WAY DOOR IS THE CHEAPEST AND STRONGEST SHORTCUT PRIMITIVE. Bar or jam a door so it opens only from the far side. The player recognizes, from behind, the door they could not open thirty minutes ago. This produces the strongest single moment of spatial comprehension available to a level designer, costs almost nothing to build, and requires no new key.
- PLACE THE LOCK ON THE CRITICAL PATH, NEVER IN A CUL-DE-SAC. The player should pass a locked door three to five times before they can open it. A lock hidden down a branch they visit once will not be remembered when the key arrives. Corollary: make each lock visually distinct at a glance (a carved emblem, a coloured plate, a chained handle) so it is nameable in the player's head before the map names it.
- THE DOOR AS THRESHOLD BEAT. The classic transition does three jobs at once: it masks streaming/loading, it kills the previous room's ambience and replaces it with a single isolated diegetic sound (knob, latch, hinge), and it denies any pre-visual of the next room. The RE HD Remaster contains 267 unique door animations — Capcom invested heavily in a 'loading screen' precisely because they refused to treat it as one. REmake made it holdable/skippable for repeat players; RE2R removed the transition entirely and relocated the beat INTO the door object itself: approach slowly and the door opens only halfway, doors can be nudged ajar, and enemies audibly slam against them from the far side. The lesson is that if you delete the transition you must replace the beat, not just the load.
- SIGHTLINE DENIAL MUST BE BUILT INTO THE FLOORPLAN ONCE YOU LOSE THE FIXED LENS. Fixed-camera games achieved concealment through authored framing — the shot simply excluded what you were not allowed to see. In OTS and first-person you no longer control the lens, so the occlusion has to be geometry: dogleg entries, L-corridors, blind interior corners, freestanding pillars and furniture on the room's main axis, and deep doorframes that crop the view into a fragment. Place the room's most important object OFF the entrance axis so the player must physically step in and turn to complete the reveal.
- SAVE/SAFE ROOMS SIT AT WING BOUNDARIES, ROUGHLY ONE PER 20-30 MINUTES. Put them at the mouth of a spoke, not at its dead end, so they function as a staging post for the trip in and a decompression chamber on the way out. Signal them from outside — light spilling under the door, a distinct door material or colour — so they are a visible goal the player can run toward rather than a surprise they stumble into.
- THE SAFE ROOM'S SENSORY CONTRACT IS A THREE-CHANNEL SWITCH: light, music, threat. Warm low-key lighting distinct from the rest of the level; an immediate swap to a calm, sparse musical loop (the first game's acoustic guitar and strings set the template); and an absolute guarantee of safety. RE2R sharpened this by having the safe-room theme start only when the player flips the light switch on — the player physically performs the transition from tension to relief. Keep the rooms small, cluttered and cosy: a lamp, a desk, a save device, storage, and a wall to pin things to.
- STACK VERTICALLY AND RE-ENTER FROM A NEW ELEVATION. Arriving at a known room from a floor above or below re-contextualizes it for free and is the cheapest way to make a map feel folded rather than merely large. Balconies overlooking the hub, mezzanines, and stairwells that connect three floors at once do enormous work.
- GIVE EVERY ROOM A NICKNAME. Writing on RE7 observes that players navigate by phrases like 'the room with all the corpses' rather than by cardinal directions. If a space cannot be nicknamed by a distinguishing object, event, colour or smell, it is not a room — it is a corridor, and it should either be given an identity or shortened.
- SCHEDULE ONE DELIBERATE FULL-MAP RE-TRAVERSAL AT ROUGHLY 60% COMPLETION. Send the player back through the earliest, most familiar area — now altered. It reestablishes mastery, escalates through contrast rather than through new content, and narratively rhymes the ending with the opening.
- BUDGET THE MACRO-AREAS BY PERCENTAGE, NOT BY ROOM COUNT. RE1: mansion 59 rooms / 43% of playtime, guardhouse plus courtyard 32%, laboratory 21 rooms / 25%. The later areas are smaller in room count but slower per room. Plan the ratio of area size to time-in-area explicitly, and change palette, materials and ambience at every boundary.

## Sistemas de progressao

- A FIVE-TIER LOCK TAXONOMY, ASCENDING IN ABSTRACTION. Tier 1 — NAMED KEYS IN A DECLARED SET: RE1's Sword/Armor/Shield/Helmet mansion keys, RE2's Spade/Heart/Diamond/Club playing-card keys. The set membership is itself a progress bar: the moment you hold the Spade Key you know three more exist. Keys are reusable within their tier and become discardable once spent, which is a progress signal in its own right. Tier 2 — EMBLEMS SLOTTED INTO A RECEPTACLE: RE1's Star/Wind/Sun/Moon crests, RE2R's Lion/Unicorn/Maiden medallions inserted into the Goddess Statue in the main hall to open the underground passage. A three-or-four-part gate that structures an entire act and physically anchors it in the hub. Tier 3 — TOOLS THAT CHANGE TRAVERSAL RATHER THAN OPENING ONE DOOR: RE2's crank (opens gates and shutters in multiple places), the Mechanic Jack Handle (lowers the library bookshelves), RE4R's Small Keys (eight of them, each opens any one locked drawer and is consumed). Tier 4 — KNOWLEDGE LOCKS: dial combinations, safe codes and portable-safe codes learned from files. The key lives in the player's head, weighs nothing, and cannot be lost or mis-stored. Tier 5 — FICTION-FLAVOURED LOCKS: play Moonlight Sonata on the piano, align the weathervanes, rotate the medallions, set the chess pieces. Mechanically trivial, memorably absurd — and the absurdity is what makes them recallable landmarks.
- INVENTORY SCARCITY AS A PACING DEVICE, WITH THE ITEM BOX AS A NETWORK NOT A PLACE. Limited slots force triage and manufacture return trips; the shared item box means every box is the same box, so the storage is a network the player carries in their head. Key items should be flagged, exempt or auto-discarded when spent to keep the pressure on consumables rather than on progression. The counter-example proves it: RE0 removed the item box and is near-universally cited as the series' backtracking nadir, because it forced round trips with no compression whatsoever.
- EXAMINE-TO-ROTATE AS A FIRST-CLASS VERB, NOT A FLOURISH. Every key item is a 3D object the player can spin in the hand, and the catch is on the back or the underside: a hidden panel, a maker's mark, a code, a compartment. Several key items in the series do not enter the inventory at all until the container holding them is examined, rotated and opened — the game will not count an object as found unless the player manipulates it properly. This converts a passive pickup into a micro-puzzle and teaches the transferable lesson that objects have backs, which then pays off across the whole game.
- THE READING LAYER AS THE NARRATIVE SPINE, PHYSICALLY PLACED AND OPTIONAL. Files come as paper documents, letters, reports, journals and audio recordings, each physically sited where it makes fictional sense (a desk, a corpse, a locker). They do three jobs simultaneously: carry backstory, deliver puzzle solutions and safe codes, and foreshadow locks the player has not yet reached. A persistent in-game file library allows re-reading, and a completion track (the 'Lore Explorer' record in RE2R) gives collectors a spine. Pacing heuristic drawn from the series: roughly one file per two to three rooms in dense areas, with solution-bearing files front-loaded near their corresponding locks so the player reads the answer before they meet the question.
- THE SELF-FILLING, COLOUR-CODED, LOCK-ANNOTATING MAP. RE2R's map is the single most transferable system in the series and has been called a living document rather than a static one. Rooms draw themselves as you enter. Red means the room still contains items; blue means it is cleared. Items you left behind because of inventory limits render as miniature icons in place. Environmental puzzles and safes appear as exclamation-mark icons with hover text naming the puzzle type. Most importantly, every locked door you fail to open is automatically annotated with its lock type, so the words 'spade door' appear simultaneously in the west office, the second-floor library and the waiting room — the player sees the entire shape of a pending unlock at a glance. This map is the anti-frustration valve that makes aggressive non-linearity survivable for a mainstream audience.
- SAVE-AS-RESOURCE VERSUS SAVE-AS-RITUAL. RE1 made saving a scarce economy with ink ribbons; RE2R kept ribbons only on Hardcore and made typewriters free on Standard. The modern default is free saving with the ROOM RITUAL fully intact — the walk to the typewriter, the light switch, the music, the box. The ritual, not the scarcity, is what players actually love; the safe-room theme is reportedly compelling enough that players will sprint for minutes just to sit in it.
- SHORTCUTS AS A VISIBLE, PERMANENT LEDGER. Treat each unlocked shortcut as a collectible in its own right: it is permanent, it is drawn on the map, and it measurably reduces friction. The player should be able to look at the map late-game and see a building they personally made easier to live in. This is the compounding-return curve that keeps a small map rewarding for ten hours.
- AN OPTIONAL ECONOMY THAT PAYS IN PERMANENT CAPABILITY. RE4R layers treasures, gem inlay to raise their value, a merchant, and side requests over the critical path; RE2R hides hip pouches that permanently expand inventory. The rule is that optional exploration should return permanent capability or permanent convenience, never just a score. That way thoroughness compounds instead of merely accumulating.
- CHEAP, REPEATED 'NOT YET' MARKERS. Locked drawers, chained gates, empty valve sockets, dead fuse boxes, bricked-up doorways. These are low-cost art assets scattered densely enough that the player constantly perceives unfinished business, and they read as 'come back' rather than 'never.' RE4R's eight Small Keys against a larger population of locked drawers is the clean version: the player always has a pending decision about where to spend the next one.
- KEEP ONLY TWO OR THREE PROGRESSION THREADS LIVE AT ONCE. If the player is holding five unmatched keys against six unsolved locks, the map has become a spreadsheet and the mystery has become bookkeeping. Open a thread, let it breathe for a wing, close it. The emblem-set gates (crests, medallions) work because they are one thread with three visible sub-goals, not three threads.

## Anti-padroes

- CONFUSION MASQUERADING AS MYSTERY. Mystery is knowing precisely what you cannot do yet; confusion is not knowing what you are supposed to be doing at all. The instant a player cannot NAME their current obstacle, the design has failed. The cure is mechanical, not atmospheric: annotate every encountered lock on the map with its type, make each lock visually distinct, and never rely on the player's unaided memory to connect a key to a door they saw once. RE2R's door-type annotation exists specifically because the original 1998 game's non-linearity produced exactly this failure.
- GIVING THE KEY BEFORE SHOWING THE LOCK. Reverse foreshadowing leaves the player holding an item with no referent, which converts exploration into a brute-force sweep of every door in the building. Always establish the lock first, ideally on a route the player is forced to walk repeatedly. The corollary failure is placing a lock in a cul-de-sac visited once — it will not be remembered and the key will feel arbitrary when it arrives.
- BACKTRACKING WITHOUT COMPRESSION. Backtracking is only good when the return trip is faster, different, or altered. Re-walking an identical, empty, already-looted corridor is a pure time tax and the most common way this genre fails. Every retread must be justified by at least one of: a new shortcut that shortens it, a changed environment that re-threatens it, or a new piece of knowledge that makes the player see the space differently. RE0's removal of the item box is the canonical failure — it forces round trips to the start of the game with no compression mechanism at all, and Code: Veronica is criticized on identical grounds.
- FORBIDDING BACKTRACKING IN A GAME THAT TAUGHT EXPLORATION. RE8 Village is widely criticized for sealing off Castle Dimitrescu and the other domains behind scripted events while simultaneously scattering 140-plus collectibles through them, so thorough exploration must happen during the game's most rushed, high-pressure sequences. If you build a hub-and-spoke map and reward collecting, you have made a promise that the building is a place; retracting that promise for pacing reasons reads as a betrayal, not as tension.
- DEAD ENDS THAT DO NOT PAY. Every branch must return one of four things: an item, a file, a shortcut, or a legible 'come back with X.' A branch that returns literally nothing teaches the player that exploration is unrewarded — and an exploration game cannot survive teaching that lesson even once or twice. Empty rooms are acceptable only if they are atmospheric set-pieces the player will remember and use as landmarks.
- THE LATE-GAME LINEAR COLLAPSE. The most consistent criticism across the entire series is that the final act abandons the interconnected grammar for a corridor — the laboratory, the NEST, Code: Veronica's arctic base. Analysis of RE1 notes the lab is a quarter of the runtime in only 21 rooms with markedly slower pacing. If the last hour is a hallway, the game retroactively frames all the exploration as a phase you graduated out of, which sours the memory of the whole.
- OVER-SCRIPTING THE SURPRISES. RE2R is criticized for leaning too heavily on scripted enemy spawns, so the game 'loses a lot of its bite' on subsequent playthroughs, and for enemies that never cross certain room boundaries — producing immersion-breaking moments where a stalker is visibly stuck. A space whose every surprise is a one-shot trigger has no second life; systemic, persistent, state-based changes replay far better than triggers.
- PUZZLE LOGIC WITH NO FICTIONAL ALIBI. The mansion's ludicrous piano-and-weathervane locks are forgiven because the building is established as a paranoid security apparatus rather than a home. Frictional Games' analysis makes the general point that narrative context is what elevates ordinary puzzles — the player is outsmarting a character, not solving a riddle. Without that alibi, the same puzzle reads as arbitrary busywork bolted onto a level.
- A MAP THAT UNDER-REPORTS. If the map omits something the player personally saw — a locked door, an item they walked past, a keypad — the player stops trusting the map and starts keeping notes on paper. That is not admirable dedication; it is a design failure that has offloaded a UI job onto the player's desk.
- BREAKING THE SAFE-ROOM CONTRACT CASUALLY. Allowing a threat into a save room once permanently converts every future save room from a release valve into a tense space, destroying a mechanic you spent the whole game building. It is a one-time, irreversible expenditure and should be treated as a major narrative beat with a real cost, if used at all.
- TOO MANY SIMULTANEOUS OPEN THREADS. Five unmatched keys against six pending locks turns navigation into inventory administration. The player's working memory, not the map's capacity, is the binding constraint.
- SCALE WITHOUT DENSITY. A larger building is not a better building. RE7 sustains ten to twelve hours inside essentially one house; the failure mode is padding a map with rooms that carry no key, no file, no shortcut and no landmark identity, which dilutes the hit rate of exploration and teaches the player to skim.

## Adaptacao para museu

"HOW TO KEEP THE EXACT GRAMMAR WITH NO ENEMIES AND NO DEATH — A VOLLEYBALL HISTORY MUSEUM\n\nTHE CENTRAL SUBSTITUTION: DARKNESS REPLACES THE MONSTER, AND KNOWLEDGE REPLACES THE KEY.\nHorror's engine is not the zombie — it is the unresolved. Resident Evil generates tension from three things that have nothing to do with violence: (1) you cannot see what is ahead, (2) you can see doors you cannot open, (3) the building is bigger than your understanding of it. All three survive intact without a single enemy. The two loops you must build are: DARK ROOM -> RESTORE LIGHT -> ROOM IS PERMANENTLY 'CLEARED' (this replaces combat), and READ THE MUSEUM -> LEARN A FACT -> THE FACT IS LITERALLY THE COMBINATION TO A LOCK (this replaces the key economy and makes the educational payload load-bearing rather than optional). The project already has HandTorch.tsx, OfficeLamp.tsx and InteractiveDeskDrawer.tsx — the skeleton of both loops is on disk.\n\n1. THE HUB — THE ATRIUM. A double-height entrance atrium with a grand staircase and a central plinth (the direct analogue of the RPD's Goddess Statue). Five to eight visible doors, most sealed. A wall-mounted 'You Are Here' floorplan. The torch cannot reach the atrium ceiling on first entry — the scale is unreadable in the dark, and the moment the main lights come on late in the game is the single biggest awe payoff in the build. The player passes through the atrium after every wing.\n\n2. THE WINGS — ERA GALLERIES AS SPOKES. Four or five wings, each 4-6 rooms and 15-25 minutes, each a distinct palette, material set and ambience: Holyoke / Morgan and the 1895 origins (gaslight, wood, sepia); the Olympic era from Tokyo 1964 (institutional concrete, film projectors); the FIVB and the World Championship years (mid-century international, flags, telex); Beach (sun-bleached timber, sand, open air, the one bright wing); Modern and Para volleyball (glass, LED, sitting volleyball, video). Each wing has one hero artifact, one safe room at its MOUTH, one credential reward, and one one-way shortcut back to the atrium. Total critical path 2-3 hours; full catalogue 5-6.\n\n3. THE SAFE ROOM — THE CURATOR'S OFFICE / STAFF BREAK ROOM. Reproduce the three-channel switch exactly. The room is dark when found; the player flips the desk lamp; the calm loop starts ON the switch, exactly as RE2R starts its safe-room theme on the light switch. Contents: the desk, an ARCHIVE CART (the item box, networked — any cart is the same cart, alibi'd as a dumbwaiter or pneumatic tube), a TYPEWRITER or visitor logbook (free save, ritual intact), a CORKBOARD where every document you have found is pinned as a physical thumbnail, and a MAP TABLE. Warm tungsten against the cold torchlight everywhere else. Signal it from the corridor with light spilling under the door. Never put anything unpleasant in one.\n\n4. THE DOOR BEAT — KEEP IT, IT IS ALSO A REAL TECHNICAL NEED. A WebGL/R3F museum genuinely benefits from a streaming mask (SceneWarmup.tsx already exists), so take Capcom's original bargain: hold-to-open, the camera dips to the handle, the previous gallery's ambience cuts to silence, one isolated diegetic sound (a heavy latch, a brass handle, a security door's magnetic release), then the new gallery's ambience swells. Make it skippable on the second pass, as REmake did. Crucially, the payoff on the far side must be a SIGHT, not a threat: a single spotlit hero object off the entrance axis, or a fragment of something enormous you cannot yet resolve.\n\n5. SIGHTLINE DENIAL WITHOUT A FIXED CAMERA. In first person you cannot author the shot, so author the occluder. No gallery reveals itself from its doorway: angled partition walls, freestanding vitrines placed ON the entrance axis, hanging banners, dogleg entries, cases at eye height. Use the doorframe to crop a deliberate FRAGMENT — the top third of a trophy, one panel of a mural, the corner of a scoreboard — so the player must step in and turn to complete it. This is the exact mechanism of the fixed-camera reveal, rebuilt in geometry.\n\n6. THE LOCK TAXONOMY, TRANSPOSED TIER FOR TIER.\n  Tier 1, declared key set: four DISCIPLINE BADGES (Indoor, Beach, Sitting/Para, Snow) or four FEDERATION PINS. Seeing the first tells the player three more exist. The map labels each sealed wing door 'Beach Badge door' the moment you touch it.\n  Tier 2, emblem gate: three COMMEMORATIVE MEDALS slotted into the atrium plinth. This is the act-two gate that opens the Founder's Vault beneath the atrium — a direct lift of the Goddess Statue and it works perfectly, because a museum genuinely would have a medal plinth.\n  Tier 3, traversal tools: the SERVICE KEY (opens any one locked display drawer, consumed on use — RE4R's Small Key), the BREAKER HANDLE or FUSE (restores power to an entire wing — RE2's crank), the CRATE DOLLY (shifts a shipping crate to open a permanent shortcut — the Jack Handle), the STEP LADDER, the VITRINE KEY.\n  Tier 4, knowledge locks — THIS IS THE HEART OF THE ADAPTATION: combination locks on archive cabinets whose codes are facts learned elsewhere in the museum. Set the dial to the year of the first Olympic volleyball tournament. Set the scoreboard to the score of a specific historic final. Set the net-height crank to the correct era standard to release a case. Enter a jersey number. The player CANNOT progress without having actually learned the content — and unlike a quiz, it never fails them, it simply waits. Every code must have exactly one findable in-world source, and the map must record where the player saw it.\n  Tier 5, fiction-flavoured locks: arrange six photographs in chronological order; align a slide carousel; rotate a trophy until its engraved date faces front; match three balls to their decades by stitching pattern. Absurd-but-memorable is a feature.\n\n7. EXAMINE-TO-ROTATE IS THE CORE VERB, AND IT IS WHERE THE TEACHING HAPPENS. Every artifact is a spinnable 3D object in the hand. The maker's mark, the signature, the date stamp, the panel count, the inflation-valve type, the handwriting on the BACK of the photograph — all of it is on the reverse or underside. Identifying an object logs it in the CATALOGUE and sometimes drops a code. Mirror the series rule exactly: the game does not count an object as catalogued unless you actually turned it over. This single mechanic converts passive museum-looking into active museum-reading and is the non-violent equivalent of an encounter.\n\n8. THE READING LAYER, IN THREE CLASSES AND STRICTLY CHUNKED. (a) WALL LABELS: always visible, headline plus ~40 words, free, no interaction. (b) ARCHIVE DOCUMENTS: letters, FIVB minutes, newspaper clippings, telegrams, scorecards, annotated photographs — optional, physically sited in drawers and filing cabinets, pinned to the office corkboard when found, re-readable, and one per two-to-three rooms with the code-bearing ones front-loaded near their locks. (c) ORAL HISTORY: reel-to-reel or cassette items that PLAY WHILE YOU KEEP WALKING, so the deepest content never stops the game. Add a completion track ('Full Provenance') as the collector's spine. Never build a mandatory wall of text — that is the museum-game equivalent of the late-game corridor.\n\n9. THE MAP — PORT RE2R'S ALMOST VERBATIM, IT IS THE MOST TRANSFERABLE SYSTEM IN THE SERIES. Rooms draw themselves on entry. Three states instead of two: grey outline = unlit/unvisited; AMBER = lit but artifacts uncatalogued or documents unread; BLUE = fully catalogued. Uncatalogued artifacts render as miniature icons in place. Puzzles show as '?' with hover text naming the type ('Combination cabinet — four digits'). Every locked door auto-annotates with its credential name. This one screen eliminates most of the anti-patterns at a stroke, and a paper gallery guide is a perfectly diegetic container for it.\n\n10. LIGHTING AS THE PROGRESS LANGUAGE. Unlit equals unexplored — that is the whole signal. The torch is the flashlight, and pools of light do the guiding: a single lit vitrine at the far end of a dark gallery is an irresistible 'come here.' Restoring a wing's power is the reward beat: the gallery blooms into full museum lighting, the ambience shifts from a cold drip-and-hum to a warm room tone, and the space is permanently, visibly cleared. That bloom is your zombie-clearing satisfaction with zero violence, and it is repeatable four or five times. Keep exactly one space deliberately never lit — the basement archive — so the torch's intimacy is preserved for the endgame.\n\n11. TENSION WITHOUT DANGER, FOUR SOURCES. (i) ANTICIPATION: the door beat, the cropped fragment, the sound of something large in the next room — a banner shifting in a draught, a ball rolling across a wooden floor, a projector still running in an empty gallery. (ii) CURIOSITY DEBT: a map full of amber rooms and named locks is genuine, sustained tension; unresolved-ness is the feeling, and it costs nothing to manufacture. (iii) AWE AND SCALE: the unreadable dark atrium ceiling, the moment the house lights come up. (iv) SOFT, NEVER-PUNISHING TIME STRUCTURE: a projector reel or a docent recording that plays on a loop at intervals — missing it costs nothing, you simply wait for the next cycle. Explicitly DO NOT bolt on stealth, guards, hazards, health or fail-timers. Death has no substitute and needs none; its replacement is INCOMPLETENESS, made permanently visible on the map.\n\n12. THE 60% RE-TRAVERSAL, RE-CONTEXTUALIZED RATHER THAN RE-THREATENED. After the third wing, the medals go into the plinth, the mezzanine opens, and the player walks the entire ground floor again from the balcony above — fully lit for the first time, seeing every gallery they catalogued from a new elevation, with the shortcuts they personally opened visible below them. Escalation by perspective change is the non-horror form of the Crimson Head. Reinforce it with genuine state changes: a plaque in a foreign language becomes readable once you find the translation card; an object's catalogue entry gains a second paragraph once you have read the related letter three galleries away. The second visit is materially different.\n\n13. GENRE-SPECIFIC ANTI-PATTERNS TO GUARD AGAINST. Never gate on a fact with no discoverable in-world source, and always let the map record where the fact was seen. Never seal a wing permanently — a museum that locks you out of a gallery breaks the promise more badly than RE8 did, because a museum is definitionally a place you wander. Never make the final act a corridor: the Founder's Vault must re-open onto the atrium as a loop, not run to credits. Never let a gallery be nickname-less. And never make wall text mandatory — the moment reading becomes a toll gate rather than a reward, the educational payload becomes the thing players learn to skip."

**Justificativa:** Verified by extracting three@0.185.1 from npm (published 2026-07-01) and listing src/geometries, src/extras/curves, examples/jsm/{geometries,modifiers,math,utils}. The bevel parameters are the single highest-leverage thing for 'looks good' — sharp 90-degree edges are the #1 tell of agent-authored geometry, and bevelSegments=2-3 at bevelThickness≈0.004-0.01 world units puts a specular highlight on every edge. RoundedBoxGeometry does the same for boxes with zero effort. LoftGeometry is the piece that unlocks trophies, banners, mouldings and curved display cases in one primitive — it did not exist in older three versions and is not in most tutorials.

## 1b. What procedural code renders convincingly vs. what it cannot

**Recomendacao:** GREEN (ship it, will look professionally made): architecture (walls/floors/ceilings/cornices/pilasters/skirting via ExtrudeGeometry along a Shape with a moulding profile), display cases and vitrines (RoundedBox + transmission glass + CSG-cut mullions), plinths and pedestals, trophies and cups (LatheGeometry profile + Loft handles + bevelled base), volleyballs (icosahedron/SphereGeometry + panel seams via CSG or a procedural texture), nets (InstancedMesh of thin boxes, or a single LineSegments/quad grid with alpha), banners and flags (PlaneGeometry with a vertex-shader/TSL sine displacement — a *static wavy* banner reads fine), picture frames (ExtrudeGeometry of a mitred profile along a rectangular Shape), benches, railings, signage, lighting fixtures, parquet/tiling. AMBER (achievable but expensive in agent effort): upholstered furniture (needs subdivision + a sag/wrinkle noise displacement), plants (three/addons/generators/TreeGenerator.js exists but is WebGPU/TSL-only). RED (do not attempt to hand-author): human faces, hands, hair, realistic anatomy, simulated cloth, anything that needs a UV layout an artist would draw. Do not try to fake these with CSG — the failure mode is uncanny, not charmingly stylised.

**Justificativa:** This split is dictated by the primitives available. Everything in GREEN is a surface of revolution, a swept profile, a boolean of boxes, or a grid — exactly what Lathe/Extrude/Loft/CSG produce with correct topology. Organics require a mesh whose topology follows anatomical loops; there is no API in three.js that generates that from parameters, and three-subdivide (1.1.5, last published 2023-08-03) is a Catmull-Clark smoother, not a modeller — it makes a blobby box blobbier, it does not make a face. Being honest here is the whole game: the museum should be designed so that the only 'organic' things in it are photographs and video on flat planes.

## 1c. three-bvh-csg and three-mesh-bvh — current state and the manifold trap

**Recomendacao:** Use three-bvh-csg@0.0.18 (published 2026-02-17, MIT, peers: three>=0.179.0, three-mesh-bvh>=0.9.7) with three-mesh-bvh@0.9.13 (2026-07-18, MIT). API: `new Brush(geometry, material)`, `new Evaluator()`, `evaluator.evaluate(brushA, brushB, op, target?)` or `evaluate(a, b, [ops], [targets])`, plus `evaluateHierarchy(rootOperation, target?)` for a tree of ops. Ops: ADDITION, SUBTRACTION, REVERSE_SUBTRACTION, DIFFERENCE, INTERSECTION, HOLLOW_SUBTRACTION, HOLLOW_INTERSECTION. Evaluator flags: useGroups (default true), consolidateGroups (true), removeUnusedMaterials (true), useCDTClipping (false, experimental). CRITICAL: run CSG ONCE at build time in a Node script, not at runtime. Feed it only closed, two-manifold brushes (BoxGeometry, RoundedBoxGeometry, CylinderGeometry with caps, closed LatheGeometry) — never PlaneGeometry, never an open Lathe, never a merged geometry with duplicated coincident faces. After each op run mergeVertices() then toCreasedNormals(). Also note: CSG results use drawRange, which breaks GLTFExporter unless you convert (call `evaluator` result through a copy that respects drawRange). Separately, three-mesh-bvh is mandatory regardless of CSG: `geometry.computeBoundsTree()` + `Mesh.prototype.raycast = acceleratedRaycast` for your interaction raycasts, and it supports BatchedMesh via computeBatchedBoundsTree()/disposeBatchedBoundsTree(). It also ships a WEBGPU_API.md for running BVH queries inside TSL shaders.

**Justificativa:** Versions and peer ranges read from the npm registry directly; API and caveats from the repo README. The README states the library is '>100x faster than other BSP-based three.js CSG libraries in complex cases' but also warns that 'due to numerical precision and corner cases resulting geometry may not be correctly completely two-manifold' — i.e. chained booleans degrade. That degradation is silent and shows up as shading artefacts and holes, which is exactly the class of bug an agent cannot see. Doing CSG at build time means a human (or a screenshot test) can catch it once, and the runtime ships clean triangles with no CSG code in the bundle at all.

## 2. Procedural textures — canvas 2D + DataTexture is the pragmatic 2026 answer

**Recomendacao:** Generate every texture in a Node build script (not at runtime) and write PNG/JPEG that then goes through KTX2. Pipeline: (a) author a height/mask function h(x,y) in plain JS using simplex-noise@4.0.3, three/addons/math/SimplexNoise.js or ImprovedNoise.js, plus worley/cellular for tiles, grout, leather and concrete; (b) rasterise albedo with node-canvas or by writing a Uint8ClampedArray directly; (c) derive the normal map from h with a Sobel filter — dx = h(x+1,y)-h(x-1,y), dy = h(x,y+1)-h(x,y-1), n = normalize(vec3(-dx*strength, -dy*strength, 1)) mapped to 0..255; (d) derive roughness from a remap of h plus a second low-frequency noise so specular breaks up; (e) pack AO+roughness+metalness into R/G/B of one ORM texture (three reads aoMap from .r, roughnessMap from .g, metalnessMap from .b when they share a texture). At runtime, keep only CanvasTexture/DataTexture for cheap things (gradients, vignettes, label text via troika/Text). Set texture.colorSpace = SRGBColorSpace on albedo/emissive ONLY; normal/roughness/AO must stay NoColorSpace or they will be gamma-wrong. Set anisotropy = min(8, renderer.capabilities.getMaxAnisotropy()) on floors. For large surfaces where UV seams are painful, use tri-planar mapping — in TSL it is one call, `triplanarTexture(texX, texY, texZ, scale, positionLocal, normalLocal)`; in GLSL you must hand-write the three-projection blend.

**Justificativa:** Textures are where 'agent-made' most obviously reads as programmer art, and the fix is not more geometry — it is a height function with 3-4 octaves plus a derived normal. Doing it at build time means (i) no runtime CPU cost or jank from generating 2048² arrays, (ii) the output can go through KTX2 and get the 8× VRAM saving (measured below), which runtime CanvasTexture cannot, and (iii) the textures are cacheable/immutable at the CDN. The one thing that genuinely needs runtime generation is text and per-visitor content, which troika-three-text (0.52.5, 2026-07-24, used by drei's <Text>) handles as SDF glyphs far better than a canvas.

## 2b. TSL has built-in noise and height-to-normal nodes — a real finding

**Recomendacao:** If you go the node-material route, you do not need a third-party noise library. three/src/nodes/materialx exports (verified by grepping MaterialXNodes.js): mx_noise_float, mx_noise_vec2/3/4, mx_perlin_noise_float/vec3, mx_fractal_noise_float/vec2/vec3/vec4, mx_worley_noise_float/vec2/vec3, mx_cell_noise_float, mx_unifiednoise2d, mx_unifiednoise3d, and critically **mx_heighttonormal** (height field → tangent-space normal, in-shader, no baked normal map needed). Plus mx_ramplr/mx_ramptb/mx_ramp4, mx_splitlr/mx_splittb, mx_aastep (analytically antialiased step — the fix for shimmering procedural stripes/tiles), mx_rotate2d/3d, mx_hsvtorgb, mx_place2d/mx_transform_uv. three also ships src/nodes/procedural/Checker.js and a PMREMNode. For higher-level ready-made materials, tsl-textures@3.0.4 (MIT, 2026-06-08, Boytchev) gives marble, wood, concrete, camouflage, scratches etc. as drop-in colorNode/roughnessNode functions.

**Justificativa:** Verified by extracting the three tarball and grepping src/nodes/materialx/MaterialXNodes.js for mx_* exports. mx_heighttonormal specifically removes the biggest cost of procedural materials — you write one scalar height function and get correct shading normals for free, with no baked map, no UV layout, and no texture memory. mx_aastep matters more than it sounds: procedurally generated tile grids and stripes alias horrifically at grazing angles on a museum floor, and a plain step() cannot be mipmapped away.

## 2c. MeshPhysicalMaterial — which features to actually use in a museum

**Recomendacao:** Verified property set in r185 MeshPhysicalMaterial: clearcoat/clearcoatMap/clearcoatRoughness/clearcoatRoughnessMap/clearcoatNormalMap/clearcoatNormalScale; sheen/sheenColor/sheenColorMap/sheenRoughness/sheenRoughnessMap; transmission/transmissionMap/thickness/thicknessMap/attenuationDistance/attenuationColor/dispersion; iridescence/iridescenceMap/iridescenceIOR (default 1.3)/iridescenceThicknessRange ([100,400])/iridescenceThicknessMap; anisotropy/anisotropyRotation/anisotropyMap; specularIntensity/specularColor; ior (default 1.5). USE: clearcoat 0.6-1.0 + clearcoatRoughness 0.05-0.15 on trophies, varnished wood, lacquered plinths — it is the cheapest 'expensive' look available. sheen 0.5-1.0 + sheenColor on velvet rope, carpet, banner fabric — it fixes the flat-plastic look of cloth without cloth geometry. USE SPARINGLY: transmission for vitrine glass — it forces a transmission render pass and is the single most expensive material in the engine; cap it at 1-2 objects on screen, or use drei's <MeshTransmissionMaterial> with a low `samples`/`resolution`, or on mobile just fall back to MeshPhysicalMaterial with transmission=0, opacity≈0.12, transparent, ior 1.5, roughness 0.02. AVOID: iridescence and anisotropy in a museum — they read as 'shader demo', and each adds permutations to the shader program count.

**Justificativa:** Read directly from src/materials/MeshPhysicalMaterial.js in the 0.185.1 tarball. The transmission warning is structural, not stylistic: three renders a separate transmission pass of the scene per transmissive material batch, so N vitrines can multiply your frame cost. In a room-based museum you can almost always cheat it — real museum glass is mostly seen at near-normal incidence where a low-opacity, low-roughness, high-ior surface is visually indistinguishable at a fraction of the cost.

## 2d. TSL / WebGPU verdict for 2026 — use it, but not yet as your only path

**Recomendacao:** Do NOT rewrite this project onto WebGPURenderer/TSL right now. Stay on WebGLRenderer + MeshStandardMaterial/MeshPhysicalMaterial for the shipping build. Reasons, with numbers: (1) bundle cost — measured from the 0.185.1 tarball, three.module.min.js is 365,552 B raw / 86,854 B gzip, while three.webgpu.min.js is 667,861 B raw / **185,168 B gzip**. Switching costs +98 KB gzip before you write a line of app code. (2) coverage — caniuse currently reports ~82.2% full + ~1.5% partial ≈ 83.6% global WebGPU; Chrome 113+ desktop, Chrome 121+ on Android 12+, Safari 26+ (macOS/iOS/iPadOS/visionOS), Firefox 141 Windows / 147 macOS, but **Firefox on Android has still not shipped it** and Linux Chrome only landed Intel Gen12+ in 144 / NVIDIA-Wayland in 147. (3) the WebGL escape hatch is imperfect: WebGPURenderer does auto-fall back (verified in src/renderers/webgpu/WebGPURenderer.js — `forceWebGL` param, and it selects WebGLBackend when WebGPU is unavailable), but the alternative of bolting TSL onto the classic WebGLRenderer via three/addons/tsl/WebGLNodesHandler.js carries a limitations list written in the source header: no VSM shadows, no MRT, no transmission, no WebGPU postprocessing stack, no storage textures, fog/environment don't auto-update, instanced mesh geometry cannot be shared, and node materials can't be used with compile(). DO adopt TSL if and when you need: many dynamic point lights (see finding 4b), compute-shader particles, or the three/addons/generators/* family (CityGenerator, ForestGenerator, TerrainGenerator, TreeGenerator) — all four are WebGPU/TSL-only, verified by their `import { MeshStandardNodeMaterial } from 'three/webgpu'` headers.

**Justificativa:** 'Is TSL production-ready in 2026' has two answers. The language and compiler are production-ready — mrdoob has moved three's own shader authoring onto it, three.tsl.min.js is only 23,676 B raw / 6,652 B gzip, and the addon ecosystem (tsl-textures, ProgressiveLightMapGPU, ClusteredLighting, the generators) is now TSL-first. But for *this* project the deliverable is a portfolio museum that must open on a recruiter's phone, and a +98 KB gzip renderer plus a Firefox-Android hole buys you nothing that a museum scene needs. The exception is genuinely the lighting problem, which is the one place WebGPU changes what is possible rather than just how fast it is.

## 3. The human problem — be honest: no-avatar is the right call, and there is no procedural-human escape hatch

**Recomendacao:** Ranked, with the honest verdict on each:

(A) **PURE FIRST-PERSON, NO AVATAR — DO THIS.** Zero organic geometry. Sell embodiment instead with motion: head-bob (2 Hz vertical sine ±0.03 m coupled to velocity), a 0.5-1.0 degree roll on strafe, footstep-synced camera impulse, a slight FOV push from 70→75 when running, and 60-80 ms of camera-rotation smoothing. This is what Gone Home / Firewatch-class walking sims and essentially every WebGL museum ship. It also removes an entire class of bugs (arm IK, clipping through walls, animation blending).

(B) **Diegetic hand-substitute if you need 'presence'** — a held object rather than a hand: a torch, a clipboard, a ticket stub, a camera viewfinder frame. Rendered on camera.layers with its own near-clip PerspectiveCamera (fov ~55, near 0.01) drawn after the main scene with autoClear=false, so it never clips into walls. Geometry is all GREEN-list (cylinders, lathes, extrudes). The project already has HandTorch.tsx — that is exactly the correct instinct, keep it, do not add an arm to it.

(C) **Procedurally-posed low-poly mannequin — technically possible, licence-blocked.** mannequin-js is at 5.2.3 but is **GPL-3.0** (verified on npm) and last published 2024-10-06. GPL-3.0 is viral: linking it into your portfolio site obliges you to release the whole site under GPL-3.0. Do not use it. Writing your own articulated figure from capsules/spheres is achievable in maybe 300 lines and looks like a wooden artist's mannequin — which is *fine as a deliberate stylistic choice* (a 'posed artist mannequin' exhibit reads as intentional), but it is NOT fine as an attempt at a person.

(D) **Free CC0 sources — the pragmatic compromise, and it does not break the brief the way you fear.** Quaternius (Universal Base Characters + universal animation library) and Kenney character packs are CC0: commercial use, modification, no attribution. Poly Haven is CC0 (verified on their licence page) for HDRIs/textures/models. Mixamo is still up in 2026 and free for commercial use with a free Adobe account, but (i) you may not redistribute the raw rig/animation files as an asset pack, and (ii) Adobe has effectively stopped maintaining it — treat it as legacy and vendor the FBX/GLB into your repo rather than depending on the service. Ready Player Me is a hosted avatar service: it gives you good-looking GLB humans but adds a third-party runtime/API dependency and its own ToS, which contradicts the 'agent authors everything' goal more than a CC0 file does.

**Recommended combination:** (A) for the player, plus a handful of CC0 Quaternius mannequins ONLY if you need a 'crowd' — and if you do, treat them as a declared, documented exception in the README rather than pretending they're procedural.

**Justificativa:** This is the one section where the honest answer is that the constraint cannot be fully satisfied. Nothing in the three.js ecosystem in 2026 generates a credible human from code — the closest thing that exists is GPL-licensed and produces a wooden dummy. But the constraint mostly doesn't need satisfying: a museum is a building full of objects, and the two places humans usually appear (the player's own body, and ambient visitors) are both places where the best-looking option is to have no human at all. An empty museum after hours is a stronger aesthetic than a museum with three CC0 low-poly people T-posing in the corner, and it happens to be the cheap option too.

## 4. Lighting — bake what you can, and three.js now ships two bakers that need no Blender

**Recomendacao:** Three real options, all in-engine:

(1) **IBL with a procedurally-built environment (do this first, it is 90% of the look).** No HDRI file needed. Either `new RoomEnvironment()` + `pmremGenerator.fromScene(roomEnv, 0.04)` (three/addons/environments/RoomEnvironment.js; ColorEnvironment.js also exists for a flat tint), or — better for a museum — build your own emissive box scene and PMREM it. PMREMGenerator.fromScene signature verified in src: `fromScene(scene, sigma=0, near=0.1, far=100, options={size:256, position:Vector3})`. In R3F this is one component: `<Environment frames={1} resolution={256}>` with `<Lightformer>` children — drei's EnvironmentPortal renders the children to a cube target and PMREMs it, so your 'HDRI' is authored in JSX. Put a big soft Lightformer where each skylight/window is, a cool one opposite for fill. Set `scene.environmentIntensity` (0.6-1.0) and `environmentRotation` to art-direct.

(2) **Lightmap baking, in-browser, no Blender.** three/addons/misc/ProgressiveLightMap.js (WebGL) and ProgressiveLightMapGPU.js (WebGPU) — by zalo, both shipped in r185. Usage: construct with (renderer, res=1024), call `addObjectsToLightMap([...meshes, ...lights])` once, then `update(camera)` every frame while jittering the lights; it accumulates a texture-space lightmap using potpack for atlasing. Run this in a dev-only route, dump the atlas to PNG, and ship it as a static `lightMap` (remember: lightMap needs a second UV set, `uv1`). This gives you soft indirect bounce on a museum interior for zero runtime cost.

(3) **Light probes for position-dependent GI.** three/addons/lighting/LightProbeGrid.js is new and significant: an L2 spherical-harmonic probe grid giving position-dependent diffuse GI, baked fully GPU-resident (cubemap render → SH projection → atlas packing, zero CPU readback), stored in one RGBA WebGL3DRenderTarget. Source header states it is **WebGLRenderer-only for now** ('A version for WebGPURenderer will be added at a later point'). Also three/addons/lights/LightProbeGenerator.js for single probes from a cubemap.

**Realtime layer on top:** exactly one DirectionalLight with shadows for sun/skylight direction (mapSize 2048², normalBias 0.02-0.05, tight shadow camera frustum around the current room only), plus 0-2 shadowless SpotLights for picture lighting using an emissive quad to sell the fixture. Shadow types available: BasicShadowMap=0, PCFShadowMap=1, PCFSoftShadowMap=2, VSMShadowMap=3 (verified in src/constants.js). LightShadow defaults are mapSize 512×512, radius 1, blurSamples 8, bias 0, normalBias 0, autoUpdate true — set `light.shadow.autoUpdate = false` and flip `needsUpdate = true` only when something moves (drei's <BakeShadows> does this). For contact/ambient occlusion darkening use drei <ContactShadows> or <AccumulativeShadows temporal frames={60} resolution={1024}> + <RandomizedLight amount={8} radius={4}> — that gives you area-light-quality soft shadows baked over the first second and then free forever. drei's softShadows({size, samples, focus}) injects PCSS into the shadow shader; it is beautiful and it is not cheap — desktop only.

**Justificativa:** All module paths, class names and signatures verified by extracting three@0.185.1 and reading examples/jsm/{environments,misc,lighting,lights} and src/extras/PMREMGenerator.js. The headline is that the 'you need Blender to bake a museum' assumption is out of date: ProgressiveLightMap has been in three for a while but the GPU variant and LightProbeGrid are recent, and between them a coding agent can produce baked GI without ever leaving JS. The Environment+Lightformer trick is the highest ratio of visual payoff to code in the whole stack — it replaces a 2-8 MB .hdr download with ~30 lines of JSX and a 256² PMREM.

## 4b. The specific cost of many point lights — measured, not guessed

**Recomendacao:** HARD RULES for WebGLRenderer (which is what you should ship): **zero shadow-casting PointLights.** A shadow-casting PointLight costs **6 full shadow-map render passes per frame** — verified in src/renderers/webgl/WebGLShadowMap.js: it allocates a `WebGLCubeRenderTarget` and the loop is literally `const faceCount = shadow.map.isWebGLCubeRenderTarget ? 6 : 1; for (let face = 0; face < faceCount; face++)`. Three shadowed point lights = 18 extra scene traversals + 18 draw-call sets per frame. Budget instead: 1 shadow-casting DirectionalLight, ≤2 shadow-casting SpotLights at 1024², and up to ~6-8 *shadowless* point/spot lights per visible room. Beyond that, three's forward renderer evaluates every light per fragment and recompiles the program whenever the light count changes (a visible hitch). Fake the rest: emissive materials on the fixture itself, a baked lightmap for the pool of light on the floor, and a cheap additive 'glow' quad. **If you genuinely need dozens of dynamic point lights**, that is the one scenario that justifies WebGPU: three/addons/lighting/ClusteredLighting.js implements Forward+ clustered shading (`new ClusteredLighting(maxLights=1024, tileSize=32, zSlices=24, maxLightsPerCluster=64); renderer.lighting = lighting;`), and its own doc string says it is 'suitable for 3D scenes with many point lights and real depth complexity'. It is WebGPURenderer-only.

**Justificativa:** The 6× cost of point-light shadows is the most commonly-missed performance cliff in three.js and it is trivially verifiable in the source, so it is worth stating as a measured fact rather than folklore. It also directly changes museum design: you want wall-washer spots and skylights, not chandeliers with shadowed bulbs. The ClusteredLighting addon is the honest counter-argument for WebGPU and is worth flagging because if the museum concept later grows into 'every exhibit has its own light', the WebGL forward path simply will not get there and the migration is a renderer swap, not a rewrite.

## 5. Performance — draw calls dominate, and BatchedMesh has no LOD in r185

**Recomendacao:** Batching ladder, in order of preference: (1) **merge at build time** — BufferGeometryUtils.mergeGeometries() for everything static that shares a material; this is free and beats every runtime trick. (2) **InstancedMesh / drei <Instances><Instance/></Instances>** for N copies of one geometry+material (benches, spotlights, floor tiles, net posts); drei also exports <Merged>, createInstances() and <InstancedAttribute> for per-instance data. (3) **BatchedMesh** for many *different* geometries sharing one material — r185 signature verified in src/objects/BatchedMesh.js: `new BatchedMesh(maxInstanceCount, maxVertexCount, maxIndexCount = maxVertexCount*2, material)`, methods addGeometry(geometry, reservedVertexCount=-1, reservedIndexCount=-1), addInstance(geometryId), setGeometryAt, deleteGeometry, deleteInstance, setMatrixAt, setColorAt, setVisibleAt, setGeometrySize, optimize(), computeBoundingBox/Sphere; properties perObjectFrustumCulled=true and sortObjects=true by default. **IMPORTANT CORRECTION to the common claim: BatchedMesh in r185 has NO LOD API** — grepping the entire file for 'lod' returns 0 hits. Use THREE.LOD / drei <Detailed distances={[0,12,30]} hysteresis={0.1}> separately. There is also an experimental three/addons/utils/SceneOptimizer.js that auto-converts a scene's meshes into BatchedMeshes ('an experimental attempt to implement auto-batching in three.js') — worth trying, not worth depending on. Culling: three frustum-culls per-object by bounding sphere automatically; there is no built-in occlusion culling. For a room-based museum, implement **portal/cell culling yourself** — it is ~80 lines and beats any general solution: give each room an AABB and a list of doorway portals, do a BFS from the room containing the camera, clip the frustum through each portal quad, and `group.visible = false` everything not reached. Set `frustumCulled = false` only on the always-visible player-held objects.

**Justificativa:** Every API detail here is read from the r185 source in the tarball, and the BatchedMesh-LOD point matters because AI-summarised docs (including one I fetched during this research) confidently hallucinate an `addGeometryLOD()` method that does not exist in this version — building on it would fail at runtime. The portal-culling recommendation follows from the fact that a museum is the textbook cell-and-portal case: rooms are convex-ish boxes connected by small openings, so a 10-room museum reduces to 1-3 visible rooms almost always, which is a 3-10× reduction in both draw calls and triangles for trivial code. R3F's own scaling-performance doc sets the ceiling at 'under 1,000 draw calls, ideally a few hundred'; the per-platform numbers I recommend are in the budgets list.

## 5b. Room streaming and memory hygiene for a 10-room museum

**Recomendacao:** Do NOT keep 10 rooms in memory. Architecture: one GLB per room (plus one shared 'kit' GLB for repeated props), loaded on demand. Use React.lazy + Suspense per room component, drei <Preload all /> only for the current + adjacent rooms, and prefetch the neighbours of the current room as soon as the player enters (a doorway trigger volume is the cue). On unload you MUST dispose manually — React unmounting does not free GPU memory for anything you created outside R3F's reconciler: walk the room group and call geometry.dispose(), material.dispose() and texture.dispose() on every unique resource, then `useGLTF.clear(url)` to drop drei's cache entry, then `renderer.renderLists.dispose()`. Verify with renderer.info: `info.memory.geometries`, `info.memory.textures`, `info.programs.length` must return to baseline after a round trip; `info.render.calls` and `info.render.triangles` are your per-frame counters (fields verified in src/renderers/webgl/WebGLInfo.js). Ship a dev-only HUD that prints these four numbers — the project already has PerfDiagnostics.tsx, extend it. Also: shader program count is a hidden memory and hitch cost; every unique material/defines combination compiles a program, so consolidate to <25 programs and warm them with `renderer.compileAsync(scene, camera)` (or drei's <Preload>) during the loading screen so the first walk-through doesn't stutter.

**Justificativa:** The dispose story is the #1 cause of 'it gets slower every time I change rooms' in R3F apps, because the framework's automatic disposal only covers objects it created via JSX props, not geometries you built imperatively in a useMemo. renderer.info is the ground truth and is cheap to read every frame. Pre-compiling matters specifically for procedural scenes: because your materials are constructed in JS rather than loaded from a GLB, they all compile lazily on first render, which is exactly when the player is walking through a doorway.

## 5c. On-demand rendering and adaptive quality

**Recomendacao:** A museum is mostly static, so use R3F's `frameloop="demand"` for menus/pause/photo-mode and `frameloop="always"` only while the player is moving — flip it in the store. When on demand, call invalidate() from your controller on any input. Layer on drei's <PerformanceMonitor onDecline={...} onIncline={...} bounds={...}> driving a quality tier, <AdaptiveDpr pixelated /> (multiplies devicePixelRatio by state.performance.current), and <AdaptiveEvents />. Cap dpr explicitly: `<Canvas dpr={[1, 2]}>` on desktop, `dpr={[0.8, 1.5]}` on mobile — uncapped dpr on a 3× phone screen is a 9× fill-rate bill and is the single most common reason an R3F scene dies on Android. Tier plan: TIER 0 (mid Android) dpr 1.0, no PCSS, no transmission, shadow map 1024², no postprocessing, ETC1S textures at 1024²; TIER 1 (good mobile / low desktop) dpr 1.25, PCF shadows 2048²; TIER 2 (desktop) dpr up to 2, softShadows, 1 transmissive vitrine, optional SSAO. Detect with drei's <DetectGPU>/useDetectGPU rather than user-agent sniffing. Use startTransition around room loads so React doesn't block the frame while instantiating hundreds of meshes.

**Justificativa:** R3F's official scaling-performance page documents frameloop="demand"/invalidate(), regress()/setDpr, AdaptiveDpr, AdaptiveEvents and PerformanceMonitor, and specifically calls out using startTransition to virtualise heavy component loads (its benchmark distributes 510 TextGeometry instances that would otherwise cause ~1.5 s of jank). For a walking-sim museum the demand loop is close to free money: standing still in front of an exhibit reading a label is the majority of session time, and rendering 0 frames during it roughly doubles laptop battery life and eliminates fan noise, which is exactly the impression a portfolio piece wants to leave.

## 6. Asset pipeline — BAKE TO GLB AT BUILD TIME (the single most important recommendation here)

**Recomendacao:** **Generate geometry procedurally in a Node script at build time, export to GLB, optimise with gltf-transform, and load the GLB at runtime.** Do NOT generate geometry at runtime in the browser. Concretely: `scripts/bake.mjs` imports three (works headless for geometry — you only need a renderer for PMREM/lightmap steps, which you can do with a headless-gl or a dev-only browser route), builds every room/prop with your Lathe/Extrude/Loft/CSG code, does mergeVertices + toCreasedNormals + computeMikkTSpaceTangents, assembles a Scene, and writes GLB via three/addons/exporters/GLTFExporter.js. Then run gltf-transform. Keep the procedural source code in the repo — it *is* the asset, the GLB is the compiled artefact — and add an `npm run bake` step plus a CI check. WHY not runtime: (a) CSG and mergeVertices on a room's worth of geometry costs hundreds of ms of main-thread jank with no way to yield; (b) runtime geometry can't be Draco/meshopt-compressed, quantized, or KTX2-textured, so it is strictly larger over the wire once you exceed trivial complexity; (c) it can't be cached by the CDN or the browser between sessions; (d) it can't be inspected, diffed, screenshotted or regression-tested. Runtime generation is correct only for: parametric things that depend on user input, LOD impostors, and small decorative geometry (<5k tris) built once inside a useMemo. Note the escape hatch: for a *small* museum you can also ship the generator code and cache the result in IndexedDB after first run — but that trades a 1-2 s first-visit cost for saving maybe 300 KB, which is a bad trade once KTX2 is in play.

**Justificativa:** This is the question the brief asks you to pick a side on, and the answer is unambiguous once you look at the compression numbers. A runtime-generated 100k-triangle room costs ~2.40 MB of Float32 vertex data (computed: 60k verts × 32 B + 100k tris × 6 B index) that must be constructed on the CPU every single page load; the same room as a meshopt-compressed, quantized GLB is on the order of 200-400 KB over the wire, decompresses in a Web Worker, and is HTTP-cached forever. The 'AI authored everything' claim is preserved perfectly — the agent wrote the generator, and the generator's output is checked in, exactly like compiled code.

## 6b. meshopt over Draco — and the decoder sizes that settle it

**Recomendacao:** Use **EXT_meshopt_compression, not Draco.** Measured decoder payloads from the three@0.185.1 tarball: meshopt_decoder.module.js is 29,256 B raw / **7,719 B gzip**. Draco (the gltf-tuned build in examples/jsm/libs/draco/gltf/) is draco_decoder.wasm 192,420 B / 63,493 B gzip PLUS draco_wasm_wrapper.js 58,456 B / 11,605 B gzip = **~75 KB gzip**. That is ~10× the decoder weight for a compression ratio that is only modestly better, and meshopt decodes several times faster. gltf-transform agrees: in @gltf-transform/cli@4.4.2 the `optimize` command's `--compress` flag defaults to **meshopt** (options: draco | meshopt | quantize | false), with `--meshopt-level high`. Recommended one-liner: `gltf-transform optimize in.glb out.glb --compress meshopt --texture-compress ktx2 --texture-size 2048 --simplify false`. Verified `optimize` defaults: --flatten true, --join true (+ --join-meshes true, --join-named true), --instance true with --instance-min 5, --palette true with --palette-min 5, --prune true (+ --prune-attributes, --prune-solid-textures), --weld true, --simplify true with --simplify-error 0.0001 (--simplify-ratio 0), --resample true, --sparse true, --texture-size 2048, --texture-compress auto, --vertex-layout interleaved. **Turn --simplify off for procedural geometry** — you control the tessellation at generation time, so let the generator emit the right density rather than letting meshoptimizer decimate your bevels. Do keep --join and --instance: those are the draw-call wins. Wire it up in three with `GLTFLoader.setMeshoptDecoder(MeshoptDecoder)`.

**Justificativa:** Decoder sizes measured directly with wc -c and gzip on the extracted tarball, so these are exact for the version you're on. The gltf-transform default flipping to meshopt is the strongest possible signal from the maintainer of the de-facto standard glTF toolchain. The --simplify caveat is specific to this project's premise and easy to get wrong: `optimize` enables simplification by default, and running it over hand-tuned procedural bevels will quietly delete the very edge loops that make the geometry look non-programmatic.

## 6c. KTX2/Basis — the 8× VRAM win, and its fixed cost

**Recomendacao:** Use KTX2 for every texture that ships in a GLB. Computed VRAM per 2048² texture including the full mip chain (×1.333): uncompressed RGBA8 = **21.33 MB**, UASTC→BC7/ASTC-4×4 (1 B/px) = **5.33 MB**, ETC1S→BC1/ETC2 (0.5 B/px) = **2.67 MB**. At 4096²: 85.33 MB / 21.33 MB / 10.67 MB. Policy: **ETC1S for albedo, AO, roughness/metalness, emissive** (`gltf-transform etc1s`, tune `--quality` before `--compression`, default compression level 1); **UASTC for normal maps only** (ETC1S mangles normals into visible banding — gltf-transform's own etc1s help text says exactly this: 'you may want to use UASTC for normal maps and ETC1S for other textures'). BUT budget the fixed cost honestly: the Basis transcoder is basis_transcoder.wasm 527,333 B / **245,397 B gzip** plus basis_transcoder.js 57,529 B / 15,084 B gzip ≈ **260 KB gzip**. That means KTX2 only pays for itself past roughly 3-4 textures — below that, ship WebP. For a 10-room museum you will be well past that threshold, so ship KTX2, load the transcoder lazily (it goes in a Worker anyway — `KTX2Loader.setWorkerLimit()`), copy the two files from `three/examples/jsm/libs/basis/` into /public rather than hotlinking a CDN, and remember `ktx2Loader.setTranscoderPath('/basis/').detectSupport(renderer)` is mandatory or the loader throws 'Missing initialization with .detectSupport(renderer)'.

**Justificativa:** The transcoder-cost caveat is the part almost every KTX2 tutorial omits, and it inverts the recommendation for small scenes. The VRAM figures are arithmetic (side² × bytes-per-pixel × 4/3) rather than quoted, so they're exact. The reason KTX2 matters more than file size is that a mid-range Android device with ~110 MB of practical GPU budget can hold five 2048² ETC1S textures (13 MB) where it could hold barely five uncompressed ones (107 MB) before the tab dies — and unlike PNG/JPEG, the GPU keeps the compressed form resident, so it's a memory win, not just a download win.

## 6d. gltfjsx is stale — write the React wrappers yourself

**Recomendacao:** gltfjsx is at 6.5.3, last published **2024-11-04** — ~20 months stale as of this research, and there is no @pmndrs/gltfjsx scope (404 on npm; @react-three/gltfjsx is a dead 4.3.4 from 2022). It still works (`--types` for TS output, `--instance`/`--instanceall` for auto-instancing, `--transform` to run the gltf-transform pipeline), but for procedurally-baked GLBs it is largely pointless: you already know your own node names because your bake script assigned them. Recommendation: skip gltfjsx. Have the bake script emit a small JSON/TS manifest alongside each GLB (node names, bounding boxes, collider primitives, interaction anchors, exhibit metadata) and write a single generic `<BakedRoom url manifest />` component that reads it. That gives you typed, stable names, and it survives regeneration — whereas re-running gltfjsx after every bake would churn a large generated file through git.

**Justificativa:** Version and publish date read from the npm registry. The deeper point is that gltfjsx exists to reverse-engineer structure out of an artist's file you didn't author; when the agent authored the file, the structure is already known at bake time and round-tripping it through a code generator loses information rather than adding it. Emitting a manifest is strictly better and is about 20 lines in the bake script.

## 7. Audio — synthesise the interactive layer, license the ambience

**Recomendacao:** Split by role. (a) **Spatialised diegetic sound** — use drei's <PositionalAudio url distance loop /> (props verified in its .d.ts: `url: string; distance?: number; loop?: boolean` on top of three's positionalAudio) attached to the emitting object; it wraps THREE.PositionalAudio and inherits setRefDistance/setRolloffFactor/setDistanceModel/setMaxDistance/setDirectionalCone. Attach one <AudioListener> to the camera. Use it for: the hum of a display case light, a ticking clock, a projector, crowd murmur bleeding from the next room — these sell room-to-room transitions better than any visual. (b) **Footsteps — synthesise them, don't sample.** A convincing footstep is a 25-45 ms noise burst through a bandpass (marble: 2-4 kHz, high Q, short decay; carpet: 300-800 Hz, low Q, longer decay; wood: 400 Hz body + a 1.5 kHz click) with ±12% random pitch/gain per step and a 2-step alternation, driven off the head-bob phase so audio and camera are locked. Implement directly with Web Audio nodes (BufferSource of white noise + BiquadFilter + GainNode envelope) — that is ~40 lines and zero bytes of download, and it lets you switch surface by raycasting the floor material. Reserve Tone.js@15.1.22 (actively maintained, 2026-07-30) only if you also want generative ambient music; it is a heavy dependency for footsteps alone. (c) **Ambience/reverb** — a single short room impulse response through a ConvolverNode, or just synthesise a decaying-noise IR (that is genuinely easy: exponentially-decaying stereo noise, 1.2-2.5 s for a stone gallery). Bus all diegetic sound through it with a wet/dry send per room. (d) **Licensing** — if you do need recorded material, Freesound filtered to CC0, Pixabay (no attribution), and Kenney's audio packs (CC0) are the safe set; Zapsplat's free tier requires attribution (CC-BY-4.0) which is a footgun for a portfolio piece. Always unlock the AudioContext on the same user gesture that requests pointer lock — browsers will not start it otherwise, and the classic bug is 'audio works on desktop, silent on iOS'.

**Justificativa:** Footstep synthesis is the highest-value item in this section and fits the brief perfectly: it is literally code-authored audio, it costs nothing to download, and it solves the problem that a looping 4-sample footstep set is instantly recognisable as canned after about twenty steps. The randomisation and the surface switch are what make it read as real. Tying it to head-bob phase rather than a timer is also what makes the camera motion feel like walking rather than floating — audio and the no-avatar decision in finding 3 reinforce each other.

## 8. Deploy on Cloudflare — the limits, and a measured 803 KB problem in your current bundle

**Recomendacao:** Limits (from developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits): Worker script **3 MB gzipped free / 10 MB paid**; static assets **20,000 files free / 100,000 paid** (the 100k tier needs wrangler ≥ 4.34.0); **25 MiB per individual asset file** — a hard ceiling, so no single GLB may exceed it (you won't come close); startup must complete in **1 second**. Serving static assets is **free and unmetered**, and there is no storage charge — only requests that actually invoke your Worker bill. Assets are tier-cached at the edge and get an automatic ETag. **The default asset header is `Cache-Control: public, max-age=0, must-revalidate`** — that is wrong for hashed 3D assets and will cost you a revalidation round-trip per file per visit. Fix it with a `_headers` file in ./dist:
```
/assets/*
  Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable
/models/*
  Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable
/basis/*
  Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable
/
  Cache-Control: public, max-age=0, must-revalidate
```
Only hash-named files get `immutable`; give the bake script content-hashed filenames. Your existing wrangler.jsonc (assets.directory ./dist, not_found_handling "404-page", observability on) is otherwise correct — leave `run_worker_first` unset so asset requests never invoke the Worker and never count against the free tier.

**Justificativa:** Numbers taken from the Cloudflare limits and static-assets docs. The default `max-age=0, must-revalidate` is the trap: it looks like caching is on because assets are edge-cached, but every browser still issues a conditional request for every GLB and KTX2 on every navigation, which on a 10-room museum is dozens of RTTs before anything renders. The `_headers` file is parsed by Workers and is not itself served, so it is a zero-risk addition.

## 8b. Code-splitting the 3D bundle — Vite 8 is Rolldown now, and Rapier is 63% of your gzip

**Recomendacao:** I built the current project (`npx vite build`) and measured the output. It emits **one chunk: 3,697,098 B raw / 1,273,720 B gzip**, and Vite warns about it. Decomposing that chunk: it contains a single base64 blob of 2,092,532 characters = **1,569,399 B of inlined WebAssembly** (this is @react-three/rapier's rapier3d-compat wasm), which alone is **803,312 B gzip / 593,284 B brotli**. Everything else — React 19 + three 0.185 + drei 10 + all your app code — is only 1,604,564 B raw / **463,053 B gzip / 353,096 B brotli**. So the physics engine is ~63% of your compressed payload and it is base64-inlined into the entry chunk, meaning it blocks first paint and compresses badly (base64 costs ~33% before compression even starts). Three actions, in order of impact: (1) **stop inlining Rapier's wasm** — either switch to `@dimforge/rapier3d` with a wasm plugin so it loads as a real `.wasm` file, or at minimum dynamic-import the whole physics subtree behind React.lazy so it lands in its own chunk fetched in parallel with the loading screen; a raw .wasm also gets streaming compilation, which base64 does not. Honestly, also reconsider whether you need Rapier at all — a museum needs capsule-vs-AABB collision against static walls, which is ~150 lines with three-mesh-bvh's shapecast() and saves the entire dependency. (2) **Split manually.** Note Vite is at **8.1.2, built on Rolldown 1.1.3** — `build.rollupOptions` is a deprecated alias and Rollup's `manualChunks` is gone; the current API is `build.rolldownOptions.output.advancedChunks` with `{ groups: [...], minSize, maxSize, minModuleSize, maxModuleSize, minShareCount, includeDependenciesRecursively }` (note: if both `advancedChunks` and `codeSplitting` are set, `advancedChunks` is ignored). Group `three` into its own long-lived chunk, `@react-three/*` into another, and each room into a lazy chunk. (3) **Route-split the experience**: ship a tiny HTML/CSS landing screen as the entry, and dynamic-import the entire <Canvas> tree on 'Enter museum'. Target ≤250 KB gzip of JS before the user clicks.

**Justificativa:** Everything here is measured from this repository rather than inferred — I ran the build and decomposed the emitted chunk byte-for-byte with zlib. The Rapier finding is the kind of thing that is invisible without doing that: the bundle looks like 'a big three.js app' but three.js is only ~87 KB gzip of it (three.module.min.js measured at 365,552 raw / 86,854 gzip). Fixing the wasm inlining is a bigger win than every geometry and texture optimisation in this report combined. The Rolldown detail matters because copying a `manualChunks` snippet from any pre-2026 article will silently do nothing on Vite 8.

## Orcamentos

- TARGET FPS — desktop: 60 fps sustained at 1440p, dpr capped [1,2], 16.6 ms frame budget with >=4 ms headroom. Flagship mobile: 60 fps at dpr 1.5. Mid-range Android (2-3 yr old, e.g. Snapdragon 6-series): 30 fps floor / 45 fps target at dpr 1.0, 22 ms frame budget.
- DRAW CALLS (renderer.info.render.calls) — desktop steady state <=120, hard ceiling 250. Mid-range Android steady state <=45, hard ceiling 100. R3F's official guidance is <1000/'a few hundred'; a portal-culled museum should sit far below that. Loading/transition frames may spike to 300 for <=3 frames.
- TRIANGLES ON SCREEN (renderer.info.render.triangles) — desktop 350,000 target / 600,000 ceiling. Mid-range Android 90,000 target / 150,000 ceiling. Per room visible: <=120,000 desktop, <=40,000 mobile. Shadow passes count separately: keep shadow-casting triangles <=60,000 desktop / 20,000 mobile.
- PER-OBJECT TRIANGLE BUDGET — hero exhibit (trophy, display case): 8,000-15,000. Standard prop (bench, plinth, frame): 500-2,500. Architectural shell per room: 15,000-30,000. Instanced filler (floor tiles, net cords, ceiling lights): <=300 each.
- GEOMETRY VRAM (computed: 32 B/vertex for pos+normal+uv f32, 6 B/tri uint16 index; ~0.6 verts per tri) — 100k tris = 2.40 MB, 250k = 7.44 MB, 500k = 14.88 MB, 1M = 29.75 MB. With KHR_mesh_quantization (14 B/vertex): 100k tris = 1.95 MB, 500k = 9.73 MB. Budget: 25 MB resident geometry desktop, 8 MB mobile.
- TEXTURE VRAM per texture, full mip chain included (x1.333) — 2048^2: RGBA8 21.33 MB / UASTC->BC7 5.33 MB / ETC1S->BC1 2.67 MB. 1024^2: 5.33 / 1.33 / 0.67 MB. 4096^2: 85.33 / 21.33 / 10.67 MB. 512^2: 1.33 / 0.33 / 0.17 MB. KTX2 ETC1S is an 8x saving over RGBA8.
- TOTAL TEXTURE VRAM — desktop <=120 MB, mid-range Android <=45 MB. Practical mobile allocation: 12 x 2048^2 ETC1S (32 MB) + 4 x 2048^2 UASTC normals (21 MB) is already over; plan 8 x 2048^2 ETC1S + 3 x 1024^2 UASTC ~= 25 MB.
- TOTAL GPU MEMORY — desktop <=250 MB, mid-range Android <=110 MB (tab-kill risk above ~150 MB). Verify via renderer.info.memory.{geometries,textures} returning to baseline after a room round-trip.
- MAX TEXTURE SIZE POLICY — 2048^2 for floors and hero exhibit albedo; 1024^2 for props and walls; 512^2 for small props and UI. gltf-transform optimize --texture-size 2048. Never ship 4096^2 to mobile.
- SHADER PROGRAMS (renderer.info.programs.length) — <=25 unique programs total. Pre-compile all of them with renderer.compileAsync() during the loading screen; every late compile is a visible 20-120 ms hitch.
- LIGHTS PER VISIBLE ROOM (WebGL forward) — 1 shadow-casting DirectionalLight (2048^2 map, normalBias 0.02-0.05), <=2 shadow-casting SpotLights (1024^2), **0 shadow-casting PointLights** (each costs 6 full shadow render passes — verified in WebGLShadowMap.js), <=8 shadowless point/spot lights, 1 AmbientLight or HemisphereLight. Beyond ~12 total lights, move to baked lightmaps or WebGPU ClusteredLighting (maxLights 1024, tileSize 32, zSlices 24, maxLightsPerCluster 64).
- SHADOW MAPS — desktop: 1 x 2048^2 directional + 2 x 1024^2 spot = ~28 MB (depth24). Mobile: 1 x 1024^2 directional only = ~4 MB. Set light.shadow.autoUpdate = false and refresh on demand. Total shadow render passes per frame: <=3 desktop, <=1 mobile.

Basis transcoder: wasm 527,333 / 245,397 gzip + js 57,529 / 15,084 gzip = ~260 KB gzip (lazy-load; only worth it past ~4 textures).
- PER-ROOM GLB (meshopt + KTX2) — <=1.5 MB target, 2.5 MB ceiling. 10-room museum total on-disk <=18 MB, but never more than 3 rooms resident at once. Shared prop kit GLB <=800 KB, loaded once.
- TIME TO FIRST INTERACTIVE FRAME — <=2.5 s on desktop cable, <=5 s on 4G mobile. First room fully streamed <=4 s desktop / <=8 s mobile. Total bytes before the player can move: <=3 MB.
- AUDIO — <=8 simultaneous PositionalAudio sources; <=1.5 MB total compressed audio (footsteps synthesised = 0 bytes); one ConvolverNode IR per room, <=200 KB or synthesised.
- CLOUDFLARE HARD LIMITS — Worker script 3 MB gzip (free) / 10 MB (paid); static assets 20,000 files (free) / 100,000 (paid, requires wrangler >=4.34.0); 25 MiB per individual file; 1 s startup CPU. Asset requests are free and unmetered. Set Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable on hashed assets via _headers (the platform default is max-age=0, must-revalidate).
- INSTANCING THRESHOLDS — use InstancedMesh at >=8 copies of the same geometry+material; use BatchedMesh at >=15 distinct geometries sharing one material; size it as new BatchedMesh(instanceCount, maxVertexCount<=200000, maxIndexCount, material) per room per material family. gltf-transform --instance-min defaults to 5.
- LOD — drei <Detailed distances={[0, 12, 30]} hysteresis={0.1}>. LOD1 at 12 m = 40% of LOD0 triangles; LOD2 at 30 m = 12%. Cull entirely beyond 45 m (rooms are portal-culled before this matters). Note: BatchedMesh in r185 has NO LOD API — grep confirms 0 occurrences of 'lod' in src/objects/BatchedMesh.js.

## Riscos

- SEARCH-RESULT POISONING: multiple high-ranking 2026 articles on three.js performance and WebGPU contain fabricated facts (e.g. 'three.js r160 shipped February 28, 2026' — r160 is from 2023). One AI doc summariser during this research also invented a BatchedMesh.addGeometryLOD() method and claimed InstancedMesh uses one draw call per instance. Verify every API against the npm tarball or threejs.org source before building on it.
- RAPIER WASM IS 63% OF THE SHIPPED PAYLOAD: measured, the current build is one 3,697,098 B chunk whose 2,092,532-char base64 blob (1,569,399 B of wasm) compresses to 803,312 B gzip. It is inlined into the entry chunk, blocks first paint, and cannot stream-compile. This is the single largest performance defect in the project today.
- CSG DEGRADATION IS SILENT: three-bvh-csg's own README warns results 'may not be correctly completely two-manifold' due to numerical precision. Chained booleans produce holes and shading artefacts that an agent cannot see without rendering. Mitigate by doing CSG at build time, capping chain depth at ~3 ops, running mergeVertices after each, and adding screenshot regression tests.
- CSG OUTPUT USES drawRange, which breaks GLTFExporter unless converted — a bake pipeline that exports CSG results straight to GLB will silently emit wrong or empty geometry.
- GPL-3.0 CONTAMINATION: mannequin-js (5.2.3) is the most discoverable 'procedural human for three.js' result and is GPL-3.0. Linking it obliges you to release the entire site under GPL-3.0.
- THE HUMAN CONSTRAINT CANNOT BE FULLY SATISFIED. No library in 2026 generates a credible human from code. If the museum design requires visible people, you must either accept CC0 assets (Quaternius/Kenney) as a documented exception or redesign so that humans appear only in photographs and video on flat planes. Attempting hand-authored organics will look uncanny, not stylised.
- PROCEDURAL GEOMETRY DEFAULTS TO PROGRAMMER ART: unbevelled 90-degree edges, perfectly uniform materials, and zero surface imperfection are the tells. Budget explicit agent effort on bevels (bevelSegments 2-3), edge wear masks, and per-instance variation — this is craft work, not a library you can install.
- MOBILE dpr IS THE #1 KILLER: uncapped devicePixelRatio on a 3x Android screen is a 9x fill-rate bill. If you ship <Canvas> without an explicit dpr cap, the mid-range Android budgets in this report are unreachable regardless of every other optimisation.
- GPU MEMORY LEAKS ON ROOM UNLOAD: R3F only auto-disposes resources it created via JSX props. Geometries and textures built imperatively in useMemo leak on every room change, producing 'gets slower each time' behaviour. Requires manual traversal + dispose + useGLTF.clear() and verification via renderer.info.memory.
- VITE 8 IS ROLLDOWN-BASED (1.1.3): Rollup's manualChunks is gone and build.rollupOptions is a deprecated alias. Any pre-2026 code-splitting snippet will silently do nothing. Use build.rolldownOptions.output.advancedChunks.groups — and note advancedChunks is ignored if codeSplitting is also set.
- CLOUDFLARE'S DEFAULT ASSET HEADER (Cache-Control: public, max-age=0, must-revalidate) forces a conditional request per asset per navigation. On a 10-room museum that is dozens of RTTs before first render. Easy to miss because edge caching makes it look like caching is working.
- KTX2's ~260 KB gzip Basis transcoder is a fixed cost that only amortises past ~4 textures; adding it to a small scene makes the site slower, not faster.
- ETC1S ON NORMAL MAPS produces visible banding — gltf-transform's own docs warn about this. Use UASTC for normals or the lighting will look subtly wrong in a way that is hard to diagnose.
- WEBGPU MIGRATION IS NOT FREE: +98 KB gzip renderer, and Firefox on Android still does not ship WebGPU (planned 2026). The WebGLNodesHandler fallback for classic WebGLRenderer explicitly does not support VSM shadows, MRT, transmission, postprocessing, or storage textures — so 'write TSL once, run everywhere' is not yet true in practice.
- SHADER COMPILATION HITCHES are worse for procedural scenes than for loaded GLBs, because materials are constructed in JS and compile lazily on first render — i.e. exactly when the player walks through a doorway. Mandatory to pre-compile during the loading screen.
- ProgressiveLightMap, LightProbeGrid, SceneOptimizer and the generators/* family are recent, lightly-documented addons (SceneOptimizer self-describes as 'an experimental attempt'). Prototype them behind a flag before committing the art direction to them.
- AUDIOCONTEXT WILL NOT START without a user gesture; the classic failure is audio working on desktop and being silent on iOS. Unlock it on the same gesture that requests pointer lock.
- iOS SAFARI remains the most memory-constrained target and will kill the tab rather than degrade. Test the 10-room streaming path on a real mid-range device, not a desktop throttle profile — throttling simulates CPU, not VRAM ceilings.

# PARTE 3 — Critica de completude

## 1. Three documents, three different museums — nobody reconciled the room count, and the arithmetic doesn't close

The brief says "one room per decade" (≈13 decades, 1895–2026). The history research delivered **6 eras**. The RE adaptation proposes **4–5 wings of 4–6 rooms**. These are three incompatible buildings and the plan must pick one before anything else is decided. My verdict: 6 eras is the only defensible unit — it's what the content research actually produced, and a decade is not a narrative unit (the 1930s and 1940s only make sense together; 2000–2026 is one continuous story).

More seriously, nobody costed the **content volume against session time**. 199 milestones ÷ 10 rooms = 20 per room; at the adaptation's own "headline plus ~40 words" that's ~800 words of wall text per room, ~4 minutes of pure reading at 200 wpm, ~40 minutes for the building. The reading layer alone consumes the entire stated 30–60 minute budget, before walking, before puzzles, before the awe beat. And the adaptation separately specifies a critical path of "2–3 hours, full catalogue 5–6" — which contradicts the 30–60 minute target by 3–6×.

Force these numbers into the plan explicitly: **6 wings; ≤8 wall labels per gallery (the other ~25 milestones per era live in the optional archive layer); 6-minute first impression, 20-minute critical path, 45-minute completionist.** A portfolio link shared on LinkedIn or WhatsApp has a median dwell time of a few minutes. Design the first 90 seconds as the actual product and the rest as depth for the 5% who stay.

## 2. The museum has no images, and no research addressed the rights problem

This is the largest unexamined hole. The tech research resolves the human-figure problem by declaring that "humans appear only in photographs and video on flat planes" — and then never asks where those photographs come from. Every one of them is a copyrighted archival photograph: 1964 Tokyo, Soviet-era championships, FIVB proceedings, 1996 Atlanta beach, contemporary VNL. An AI agent cannot author them and you cannot ship them. FIVB and national federation logos, team crests, player likenesses and jersey designs are all separately encumbered. A "Volleyball Museum" whose walls are empty frames is not a museum.

There are only four honest routes and the plan must commit to one in writing: (a) **public domain by age** — US works published before 1931 are PD in 2026, which covers the origins wing and nothing else; (b) **Wikimedia Commons / CC-BY with a visible credits wall** — a diegetically perfect solution (museums credit their lenders) but requires a per-image provenance field in the content schema and an audit; (c) **abstraction as art direction** — no photographs at all; every "image" is a procedurally generated halftone, contour reconstruction, silhouette diagram, or data visualization authored by the agent, which is on-brief, legally clean, visually distinctive, and turns the constraint into the identity; (d) commission/purchase, which is out of scope. Route (c) plus (a) is the one I'd take, and it should be decided *first*, because it determines the art direction of every wall in the building.

## 3. Facts are load-bearing, and the fact pipeline has a ~50% pre-correction error rate

The adaptation's central idea — knowledge locks where the code *is* a historical fact — makes accuracy a **functional dependency, not an editorial nicety**. A wrong year is now a soft-lock. The era research reports 109 corrections across 199 milestones from the adversarial pass. Nothing in the research specifies a canonical fact store, a citation field, a confidence rating, or a re-verification workflow, and no verified fact list was actually handed over — only headlines and counts. The plan needs a `facts.json` with `{id, claim, value, sources[], confidence, verifiedAt, usedAsCode: boolean}`, a rule that **no fact below "two independent sources" may be used as a lock code**, and a build-time check that every lock's answer resolves to a verified fact id.

The related design failure: a knowledge lock with no escape is a quiz that fails the casual visitor. Every one needs escalating in-world hints on a timer (the docent recording repeats the year; the plaque's relevant line highlights after 45 seconds; the third failed attempt reveals a nudge), so the lock reads as *"go read that panel"* rather than *"you may not enter."* Otherwise the educational payload becomes a toll gate — which the adaptation itself names as the fatal anti-pattern, and then builds its entire Tier-4 lock system on top of.

## 4. "One room per decade" structurally destroys the RE spiral

Chronology is a line. RE's grammar is a spiral: keys found in accessible space unlock doors in *already-visited* space. If gallery N+1 is decade N+1, every credential can only point forward, there are no loops, no meaningful backtracking, no one-way shortcuts that matter, and you have shipped the "late-game linear collapse" from the first minute — a chronological corridor with doors in it. Simultaneously, if the player *must* proceed chronologically, a code learned in the 2000s wing can never gate a 1930s cabinet, which is precisely the connective tissue that makes the map feel folded.

The fix is to **decouple the building's topology from chronology**: an atrium hub with era wings the player may enter in any order, and a second, cross-cutting axis — the ball, the net, the rules, the officials, the beach, sitting volleyball — whose exhibits are distributed across eras so that finishing a *theme* requires visiting several eras. Chronology becomes the content ordering; the theme threads become the spatial spiral. That also solves the repetition problem: ten "gallery with vitrines and wall panels" rooms in sequence is the same room ten times regardless of palette.

## 5. Where the RE grammar genuinely fights the museum — a blunt verdict

Keep: darkness-as-unexplored, the RE2R map verbatim, examine-to-rotate as the core verb, one-way shortcuts, nickname-able spaces, the door beat, the sensory contract of the safe room, sightline denial through geometry.

Cut or invert: **inventory scarcity and the item-box network** (a solution to a problem you don't have; it adds only friction and UI); **the safe-room cadence** (one per 20–30 min means exactly one in a 45-minute museum — make the Curator's Office a single hub-adjacent landmark, not a repeated pattern); **the 60% full-map re-traversal** (at 45 minutes total this is minute 27, and it reads as padding); and most importantly **the withholding posture itself**. A locked door is a bounce in a portfolio piece and an institutional contradiction in a museum. Invert the polarity: **the galleries are open from the start; the ARCHIVE is locked.** Progression gates the *depth layer* (the vault, the provenance, the documents, the ending), never the exhibits. That preserves curiosity debt, keeps the map full of named locks, and means a visitor who plays for four minutes still saw a museum rather than a dark building that refused them.

Also note that three of the adaptation's four tension sources need session length to work. Curiosity debt at minute three just reads as "the lights are broken."

## 6. Systems nobody researched at all

**Content authoring format.** The single most important architectural decision in the project and it appears nowhere. The existing codebase's actual disease — geometry, decor and furniture positions as hardcoded constants inside 923-line components — will reproduce at 10× scale unless a room becomes a *data file* consumed by a generic generator: room shell dimensions, a kit-part placement list, exhibit records (id, era, artifact geometry recipe, label text keys, examine hotspots, catalogue entry, unlock effects), document records, lock records, audio emitters. The bake script reads it, the runtime reads the emitted manifest, and adding gallery seven is authoring JSON, not writing a component.

**i18n PT/EN.** `index.html` is `lang="pt-BR"`, the README is Portuguese, and the stated destination is the "Players On" site — this is a Brazilian-audience product and English is the portfolio-reach language. Nobody mentioned it, and it collides directly with the bake pipeline: **you cannot bake label text into GLB geometry if there are two languages.** All text must be runtime troika SDF, which changes the draw-call and font-atlas budget; Portuguese needs the full Latin-1 diacritic range in the atlas; PT strings run 15–25% longer than EN and will overflow physically-sized label plaques unless the layout auto-fits; and any knowledge lock whose answer is a *word* rather than a number breaks across languages. Decide now, not after 200 labels exist.

**Save/persistence.** The store has zero persistence today. Specify: localStorage (no account, no backend, LGPD-trivial) with a **versioned schema and a migration/discard path** — content ids *will* change, and a save referencing a deleted exhibit must degrade, not crash. Decide whether the RE "typewriter ritual" survives at all; for a web museum, silent autosave-on-room-change plus an explicit "Continue" on the title screen is almost certainly correct, and the diegetic logbook can be a *view* of that state rather than the mechanism.

**Accessibility.** A dark, torch-lit, pointer-locked first-person walk is close to the worst-case starting point. Non-negotiables: head-bob and FOV-push **off by default or one toggle away** (the tech research recommends both as embodiment sells; they are also the top vestibular triggers), a movement-speed and turn-smoothing slider, a global brightness/gamma control (the entire progression language is "unlit," which is unreadable on a phone in daylight), WCAG AA contrast on all label text, no red/green as a state channel on the map, full keyboard operability of every interaction including examine-to-rotate, subtitles for all oral-history audio, and — the one that also buys you SEO and a no-WebGL fallback — a **complete static HTML "reading mode"** of the museum's entire text content at a real URL. That single page answers accessibility, search indexing, the "recruiter on a locked-down laptop" case, and the low-end-device bail-out simultaneously.

**Analytics.** Cloudflare Web Analytics is free, cookieless and LGPD-clean. Instrument: entry, first-move, per-room enter/exit timestamps, exhibits examined, locks attempted vs solved, quit point. Without it you cannot tell whether the design works, and "where do they quit" is the only question that matters for a piece like this.

**QA of a 3D game built by an agent that cannot see.** Genuinely the biggest tech gap, and remarkable given the premise. Needed: a **lock-graph solvability validator** (topological check that every lock's key/fact precedes it on some reachable path, no cycles, no orphan credentials — ~100 lines, catches the class of bug that ruins the whole experience); a bake-time geometry validator (manifold check after each CSG op, degenerate/NaN triangles, bounding-box sanity, tri-count vs budget per asset); deterministic **screenshot contact sheets** — fixed camera rigs per room rendered headless in CI and diffed, which is the only way an agent perceives its own art; a scripted headless playthrough bot that walks the critical path and asserts completion; and a **perf gate in CI** reading `renderer.info` (draw calls, triangles, programs, memory before/after a room round-trip) that fails the build on regression. Nothing else gives the agent a feedback loop.

**Art direction as a system, not per-room taste.** Specify one kit — wall module width, ceiling height, door width, vitrine family, plinth family, label family, bevel profile, a shared material library of ≤12 materials — and let each era vary **only palette, light temperature, floor material and hero prop**. And settle the fundamental fork nobody named: is the 1930s gallery *styled like the 1930s* (period pastiche, ten different visual languages, ten times the art cost, high drift risk) or is it *a contemporary museum room about the 1930s* (one coherent institutional language, era expressed through content and accent)? The second is cheaper, more coherent, more credible, and more achievable procedurally. Pick it, and let exactly one wing break the rule for contrast (the beach wing).

**Narrative POV.** 199 milestones is an encyclopedia. There is no voice, no character, no reason you are here at night with a torch. The adaptation itself says puzzles need a fictional alibi. Supply one: *you are the incoming curator the night before reopening — restore the power, re-catalogue the collection, find what your predecessor left in the vault.* That single sentence justifies darkness, drawers, the catalogue, the documents, the safe room and the ending, and costs nothing.

## 7. Concrete gaps in the tech research

The Rapier finding is excellent and correct, but "replace it with ~150 lines of three-mesh-bvh shapecast" is an unvalidated assertion that has to carry step-up, slopes, stairs, crouch, the existing scripted sit, and mobile-frame-rate-independent stepping. Prove it in the slice or keep Rapier lazy-chunked; do not discover it in month two.

Missing entirely: the **interaction system** (raycast targeting, prompt UX, hold-vs-tap, the examine-to-rotate camera and its exit), and its **mobile control scheme** — there is no pointer lock on mobile, and the core verb of the entire adaptation ("spin the artifact and read the underside") has no specified touch design, despite mobile being the majority of traffic for a shared link.

Also missing: **bake determinism and iteration cost.** Every procedural generator must be seeded, or each bake churns every GLB and CI diffs become noise; the bake needs per-asset caching keyed by source hash, or a full CSG bake will exceed the agent's usable iteration loop; and committing binary GLBs to git will bloat the repo — decide between LFS and build-in-CI-only. Nothing specifies who runs the PMREM/lightmap bake, which needs a GPU and is therefore not CI-able as described.

Finally, the deployment research completely misses the stated destination: this is meant to **embed into the Players On site**. In an iframe, pointer lock requires `allow="pointer-lock"` and fullscreen requires `allow="fullscreen"` on the parent's iframe tag, WebAudio unlock needs a gesture inside the frame, and the parent's CSP `frame-src`/`connect-src` must permit the worker origin. That is a five-minute fix that silently breaks everything if discovered late.

## 8. The smallest vertical slice

Not one room. **One hub plus one wing, with every system end-to-end and the CI gates in place** — because the risk is never the geometry, it is the twelve systems that have to coexist.

Build: the **atrium** (hub, four visible doors of which three are sealed, the plinth, the unresolvable dark ceiling, and the lights-on moment) + **one gallery** + **the Curator's Office** + **one corridor with a one-way shortcut back**. Four spaces. Choose the gallery as whichever era is most dependent on archival imagery, so the rights decision from §2 is forced immediately rather than deferred.

Inside it: three exhibits with examine-to-rotate (one only catalogues if actually rotated); one credential door, one knowledge lock with the full hint-escalation ladder, one locked drawer; the map with all three states and auto-annotated locks; the catalogue; save/resume with a version field; a settings panel with language, motion, brightness and quality; synthesized footsteps with surface switching; one positional audio source; the door beat; the reading-mode static page. All content in PT and EN from one data file. The bake script emitting two GLBs plus a manifest through meshopt + KTX2, `_headers` deployed, Rapier removed or lazy-chunked, the dev HUD extended.

Gate it on measurements, not vibes: ≤250 KB gzip before the click, ≤2.5 s to first interactive frame on desktop / ≤8 s on 4G, 60 fps desktop and ≥30 fps on a **real** mid-range Android (not a throttle profile), `renderer.info.memory` returning to baseline after a room round-trip, the lock graph validating, the contact sheet rendering, and — the only test that matters — **a person who has never seen it completing the wing in under eight minutes and being able to say one true thing they learned about volleyball afterwards.**

If that slice costs more than roughly a fifth of your total budget, the six-wing museum is not happening and the plan should say so in advance: cut to three wings and make them excellent.
