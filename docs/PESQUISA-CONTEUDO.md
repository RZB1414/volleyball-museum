## 1895–1929 — The Origins: Mintonette, the YMCA and the First Codification

**Volleyball was born in a Massachusetts mill-town YMCA gym as a deliberately gentle indoor pastime for middle-aged businessmen, and in barely thirty years the YMCA's global missionary network and the troopships of the First World War turned that improvised parlour game into a codified, three-hit, six-a-side attacking sport played on four continents.**

Outside the windows: the canal-and-mill townscape of Holyoke — brick mills, smokestacks, gaslight standards, snow.

### Objetos de acervo (8)

#### The original Spalding laced leather volleyball (c. 1900–1920)

A sphere of 25–27 in circumference — i.e. 203–218 mm diameter, model at 210 mm — weighing 9–12 oz (255–340 g), so it should read as noticeably smaller and denser than a basketball. THREE LAYERS: (1) innermost, a latex rubber bladder of bicycle-inner-tube stock, dull grey-tan, with a short tie-off neck; (2) a cheesecloth/canvas casing woven roughly 8×5 threads per cm; (3) an outer skin of full-grain tanned cowhide. PANELLING: period balls used elongated rectangular panels with rounded ends laid in bands around the sphere; sources disagree between 8, 12 and 18 panels — the safest read for a hero prop is 12 panels arranged as 6 bands of 2, or 18 as 6 bands of 3. Panels are joined by RAISED OUTSEAMS: the leather edges are turned out and hand-stitched with waxed linen thread, producing a welted ridge standing ~1.5–2.5 mm proud of the surface, with visible stitch pricks every ~4 mm. LACING: one panel carries a 75–100 mm slit closed by a flat rawhide lace threaded through 8–10 punched eyelets, cross-laced and the knot tucked under; this patch sits very slightly flat and is the ball's darkest, most polished area. Pre-1924 balls have NO valve button — inflation required unlacing. COLOUR: fresh russet/harness tan (#C9A06A) ageing to a mottled walnut brown (#8B5A2B) with darker grime in the seams and pale abraded highlights on the crown of each panel. FINISH: matte to low satin, fine pebble grain, no gloss. MARKING: an oval or shield stamp in black or worn gold reading 'A.G. SPALDING & BROS.' with 'OFFICIAL VOLLEY BALL' beneath, hot-stamped into one panel and partly rubbed away.

*Por que importa:* This is the single most recognisable artefact of the era and the direct ancestor of every volleyball since. Its weight, its hard raised seams and its rawhide lace explain why the early game had no diving defence and why the 1895 rules let women catch the ball to protect their fingers.

#### The improvised first ball: a laced leather basketball and its bare bladder

TWO objects displayed together. (a) An 1890s laced basketball: sphere of ~30 in circumference (~240 mm diameter), built from EIGHT elongated leather panels in a classic 4-over-4 arrangement, thick raised outseams stitched in heavy waxed cord, a 100 mm laced slit with a chunky rawhide thong, dark saddle-brown leather (#6E4423) polished to a low sheen at the equator. Visibly larger and heavier-looking than the volleyball beside it. (b) The bare bladder: an irregular, slightly pear-shaped rubber sac ~200 mm across, dull grey-buff (#A79880), semi-matte with a faintly greasy sheen, surface finely wrinkled and pleated where it is under-inflated, one gathered neck ~40 mm long pinched shut with a whipped cord tie. It should sag and read as too soft.

*Por que importa:* Morgan's own documented false starts. The basketball was 'a little too heavy'; the bladder alone was 'too soft'. Showing both is how a museum tells the story of why Spalding was ever asked to build a new ball at all.

#### The 1897-specification net and its posts

NET: 27 ft (8.23 m) long and at least 2 ft (0.61 m) deep — a startlingly shallow band compared to today's 1 m net. Top line exactly 6 ft 6 in (1.98 m) from the floor (raise to 7 ft 6 in / 2.29 m for a post-1900 variant). Body is knotted square mesh in tarred or natural hemp/cotton cord ~3 mm thick, mesh openings ~90–100 mm, colour ecru to dirty straw (#C8B78E) darkening to olive-brown where handled. Top edge is a doubled canvas duck tape band 50–75 mm wide, off-white to oatmeal (#E4DCC6), machine-stitched along both edges, with a 10 mm hemp rope run through it and emerging at each end as a spliced eye. Bottom edge is a plain roped hem, no tape. NO antennae, NO side bands, NO sponsor panel — none existed. The net sags visibly in the middle; period nets were tensioned by hand. POSTS: square-section turned hardwood uprights (ash or maple) roughly 70–80 mm square, ~2.4 m tall, chamfered corners, finished in dark amber varnish scuffed to bare wood at ankle height, seated in cast-iron floor sockets with a four-bolt flange plate, and fitted with a plain iron cleat and a ring or single-sheave pulley near the top for the tensioning rope. In many period gymnasia there were no posts at all: the net's end ropes were simply hitched to iron wall hooks or to the ladders and stall bars, which is the most authentic staging for the 1895 Holyoke room.

*Por que importa:* The 6 ft 6 in height was chosen as 'half a foot taller than the average man' — the whole physical logic of the original game. A shallow, sagging hemp net with no antennae is the fastest visual cue that a player is standing in the 1890s and not in a modern gym.

#### The 1897 Official Handbook of the Athletic League of the Y.M.C.A. of North America

A pocket-sized booklet, roughly 5 × 7.5 in (127 × 190 mm), 8–12 mm thick, side-stapled or saddle-stitched. Cover is a soft coated paper wrapper in oatmeal, sage or ochre, printed in a single black ink: a double-rule border box, a heavy condensed slab-serif title in all caps with wide letterspacing, an ornamented Victorian sub-line, and the Spalding trademark (a stitched-baseball roundel) bottom centre. Edges foxed and dog-eared, spine cracked, one corner soft. Interior is cream-to-tan newsprint stock with a serif text face, generous leading, running heads, and a single crude line-drawing of the 25 × 50 ft court with the 4 ft dribbling lines dashed on either side of the net. When modelled open, the page spread should be the 'Volley Ball' rules with the numbered rule paragraphs visible.

*Por que importa:* This is the object that turns an invention into a sport. Every measurement a plaque can honestly quote — 25 × 50 ft, 6 ft 6 in, 25–27 in, 9–12 oz, nine innings — comes from this book.

#### The Spalding Athletic Library 'Volley Ball Guide', 1916–17 edition

Same pocket format, ~5 × 7 in, ~64–96 pages, but visually louder than the 1897 handbook: a saturated Spalding-red or cobalt paper cover (#C0202B or #1F3E7C) printed in black and white, with the Spalding stitched-baseball logo top centre, a halftone action photograph or a line illustration of a player reaching for a ball in an oval vignette, the series line 'SPALDING'S ATHLETIC LIBRARY' in a condensed gothic across the head, and 'PRICE 10 CENTS' in a small starburst or corner box. Interior: coarse-screen halftone photographs of YMCA teams in rows, statistical tables, and advertising pages for Spalding balls, nets and gymnasium suits set in mixed Victorian display faces.

*Por que importa:* It is the era's mass-media object. It carries Robert C. Cubbon's 1916 estimate of 200,000 American players, and it is where Morgan himself put on record the credit due to Dr. Frank Wood and John Lynch.

#### The 1895–1915 gymnasium suit

TWO variants, both correct for the period. (a) The businessman-player of 1895: collarless white or ecru cotton shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow, tucked into dark high-waisted wool trousers held by braces or a narrow leather belt; a waistcoat and moustache optional; plain low leather shoes. (b) The catalogue athlete of c. 1901–1915 (the Spalding gymnasium suit): a ribbed knit worsted-wool or cotton jersey in either sleeveless-tank or quarter-sleeve cut, snug through the chest, colours cream, white, navy (#1F2A44), maroon (#6B222B) or black, sometimes with one or two contrasting chest stripes; knee-length knit tights or knee-length wool-flannel pants in black or navy with a contrast stripe down the outer seam; a wide woven web belt 40–50 mm with a two-piece metal clasp; long ribbed wool socks pushed down; and flat-soled lace-up canvas or soft-leather gym shoes with a smooth leather sole for sliding on waxed maple (suction-cup rubber soles arrive in 1903). Everything is heavy, matte, slightly fuzzy wool — model with high roughness, visible knit rib, and sag at the knees and seat. Shorts get shorter and snugger through the 1920s; shorts worn in front of women remained taboo before the 1930s.

*Por que importa:* It immediately dates the room, and it embodies Morgan's brief: this was invented for middle-aged businessmen in ordinary clothes, not for athletes. Putting a moustachioed man in braces on one side of the net and a jerseyed 1920s player on the other is the whole thirty-year story in one frame.

#### The Holyoke YMCA gymnasium (1886–1943), northwest corner of High and Appleton Streets

The room where the game was invented; the building burned in 1943, so it must be reconstructed from period YMCA typology. EXTERIOR: a four-to-five storey Victorian red-brick block (#8E3B2F brick, buff mortar) in the Richardsonian-Romanesque manner — round-arched ground-floor openings with brownstone voussoirs and impost bands, segmental-arched double-hung windows above, a corbelled brick cornice, a corner entrance arch, incised stone lettering. INTERIOR GYM: a tall rectangular hall roughly 12 × 25 m and 8–9 m to the truss, sized to swallow the 25 × 50 ft court. Floor is narrow-strip hard maple laid the long way, waxed to a honey-amber (#B5793C) with a low specular sheen and scuff arcs. Walls: vertical beadboard wainscot to ~1.2 m painted deep olive or oxblood, plaster above in ochre-cream, a dark painted dado rule. High segmental-arched windows with multi-pane double-hung sash on one long wall, throwing hard dusty shafts across the floor. Roof carried on exposed riveted steel or heavy-timber trusses, painted dark, from which climbing ropes and rings are hung. A narrow ELEVATED RUNNING TRACK of varnished boards on iron brackets runs around three sides at ~4 m, with a turned-spindle wooden guardrail — a signature 1890s YMCA feature. Lighting: pendant fixtures on long stems with opal or clear glass globes, carbon-filament bulbs and surviving gas fittings, 2200–2700 K, pooled and dim. Fittings: Swedish stall bars on the end wall, a leather vaulting horse, wall-mounted racks of Indian clubs and wooden dumbbells, a cast-iron radiator, and a framed red-triangle YMCA emblem.

*Por que importa:* It is the birthplace, and it no longer exists — which makes reconstructing it the strongest possible reason for the room to exist at all. The suspended running track, the maple floor and the arched windows are the three details that make a player believe they are standing in 1895.

#### YMCA apparatus of the period: Indian clubs, wooden dumbbells, medicine ball, lacing awl and ball inflator

INDIAN CLUBS (always in pairs): turned hardwood in a long-necked bowling-pin silhouette, 350–500 mm long, bulbous body ~75 mm diameter tapering to a 25 mm neck and a rounded knob handle; natural shellac showing the grain, or black lacquer with two thin gold pinstripes near the waist. DUMBBELLS: solid turned maple, 250–300 mm long, two spheres ~80 mm joined by a 30 mm waisted grip, varnished and hand-polished dark at the grip. MEDICINE BALL: leather, ~330–360 mm diameter, built of curved panels with raised outseams and a laced patch, tan darkening to olive-brown, visibly soft and sagging. INFLATOR: a hand pump ~450 mm tall — brass or nickelled steel barrel, turned wooden T-handle, a short leather or rubber hose ending in a tapered brass nozzle (a needle would be anachronistic before 1924). LACING TOOL: a curved flat steel lacing needle or awl ~150 mm with a turned wooden handle and a brass ferrule, plus a coil of flat rawhide lace. Stage the pump, awl and lace beside an unlaced ball with its bladder half out.

*Por que importa:* These props establish that volleyball was born inside an existing physical-culture institution with its own equipment vocabulary. The awl and pump also carry a real historical point: before the 1924 needle valve, inflating a ball meant unlacing it and hauling the bladder out.

### Marcos (26)

- **1870** [high] William George Morgan is born 23 January 1870 in Lockport, New York; he dies 27 December 1942 in the same town. He met James Naismith in 1891 during a football game against Mount Hermon School, and Naismith encouraged him to attend the International YMCA Training School (now Springfield College), from which Morgan graduated in 1894.
- **1895** [high] Morgan, aged 25, becomes Physical Director of the Holyoke, Massachusetts YMCA and invents 'Mintonette' — a deliberately non-contact indoor net game for older, sedentary businessmen for whom basketball (invented four years earlier five miles away in Springfield) was too strenuous. He borrowed elements from tennis, handball, basketball, baseball and the German game Faustball.
- **1895** [high] The exact invention date is contested. The universally repeated '9 February 1895' has been challenged by the International Volleyball Hall of Fame, whose archival research found no verifiable citation for it and established that Morgan's Auburn, Maine posting ended in August 1895 and that he did not start at Holyoke until 30 August 1895. The IVHF places the invention in December 1895 ('in the winter'). Museum plaques should say '1895', not '9 February 1895'.
- **1895** [high] Morgan first strung a net at 6 ft 6 in (1.98 m) — roughly half a foot above the average man's head — across a court of 25 × 50 ft (7.6 × 15.2 m). He wrote the first ten rules with the help of Dr. Frank Wood and Holyoke fire chief John Lynch, both of whom Morgan formally credited with 'material contributions' in the 1916–17 Spalding Athletic Library Volley Ball Guide.
- **1895** [high] The first ball was improvised: Morgan tried a basketball, which was 'a little too heavy', then the bare basketball bladder, which was 'too soft'. He therefore commissioned A.G. Spalding & Bros. — who had a factory nearby in Chicopee Falls — to build a purpose-made ball: a rubber bladder encased in leather, roughly 25 inches in circumference.
- **1896** [high] On 7 July 1896 Morgan demonstrated the game in the east gymnasium of the International YMCA Training School in Springfield, at the YMCA Physical Directors' Conference convened by Luther Halsey Gulick. He brought two five-man teams from Holyoke, captained by Holyoke mayor James J. Curran and fire chief John Lynch. Professor Alfred T. Halstead, watching the volleying nature of the play, proposed renaming 'Mintonette' as 'Volley Ball'. Morgan agreed.
- **1896** [high] The first rules — only ten of them — were printed in the July 1896 issue of the American magazine 'Physical Education'. The name remained two words, 'volley ball', until 1952.
- **1897** [high] The first officially published rulebook appears in the Official Handbook of the Athletic League of the YMCA of North America (1897). Verbatim specifications: 'The court or floor space shall be 25 feet wide and 50 feet long, to be divided into two square courts, 25 by 25 feet, by the net'; a dribbling line is marked 4 feet from the net on each side; 'The net shall be at least 2 feet wide and 27 feet long' and 'The top line of the net must be 6 feet 6 inches from the floor'; 'The ball shall be a rubber bladder covered with leather or canvas. It shall measure not less than 25 inches, nor more than 27 inches in circumference, and shall weigh not less than 9 ounces nor more than 12 ounces.' A game is nine innings; a side scores only while serving; two service trials are allowed; the serve must travel at least 10 feet; net balls and line balls are failures.
- **1900** [medium] Canada becomes the first country outside the United States to take up the game. The same year Morgan leaves the YMCA for business; secondary sources also date to 1900 the arrival of the definitive purpose-built Spalding ball and the raising of the net to 7 ft 6 in.
- **1905** [medium] Volleyball reaches Cuba. Cuban sources date it precisely to a class given on 17 October 1905 by the Canadian instructor Mr. L. M. Ward in the gymnasium of the Havana YMCA, with a four-team tournament of American residents and Cubans following on 28 October 1905; an alternative Cuban account credits U.S. Army officer August York during the second military intervention. FIVB's own history gives 1906. Treat the year as 1905/1906.
- **1908** [medium] Volleyball reaches Japan, demonstrated on the YMCA courts in Tokyo by Hyozo Omori, a Springfield College graduate (class of 1907). China follows around 1910–1911, via Max Exner and J. Howard Crocker.
- **1910** [high] Elwood S. Brown, Physical Director of the Manila YMCA, introduces volleyball to the Philippines, where it spreads explosively through backyards, beaches and U.S. military garrisons.
- **1913** [medium] Volleyball is on the programme of the first Far Eastern Championship Games, opened 4 February 1913 on the Manila Carnival grounds — the sport's first appearance in an international multi-sport competition. Teams at these early Far Eastern Games fielded 16 players a side.
- **1916** [high] In the Philippines players invent the offensive combination of a high trajectory pass struck down by a second player — the set and spike. Filipinos called the kill the 'bomba' and the hitter the 'bomberino'; Americans called it the 'Filipino bomb'. This single innovation turned volleyball from a rally game into an attack-and-defend game and forced the rule changes of the following years.
- **1916** [medium] The YMCA persuades the NCAA to issue the rules jointly, pushing the game into American schools and colleges. In the 1916 Spalding Volley Ball Guide, Robert C. Cubbon estimates 200,000 American players: 70,000 in the YMCA, 50,000 in the YWCA, 25,000 in schools and 10,000 in colleges.
- **1917** [high] A game is shortened from 21 points to 15 points.
- **1918** [high] The number of players is fixed at six per side.
- **1919** [high] About 16,000 volleyballs are distributed by the American Expeditionary Forces to their own troops and to allied armies, seeding the game across Europe. FIVB's history credits the AEF and wartime YMCA secretaries with carrying the game to France (from 1915), Italy, Russia and India (1917) and the rest of Europe (1918). Volleyball was NOT contested at the 1919 Inter-Allied Games in Paris — it was judged too little known among the 18 allied nations for a balanced competition.
- **1920** [high] The two rules that define modern volleyball are instituted: a maximum of three hits per side, and a restriction on attacking from the back row — both direct responses to the Filipino spike. FIVB's own summary instead dates the three-contact maximum to 1922; American sources (NCVA, Volleyball.com, Encyclopedia.com, Wikipedia) consistently say 1920.
- **1921** [medium] The Great Japan Volleyball Association is established and joins the Great Japan Sports Association — one of the earliest national volleyball governing bodies anywhere. (The modern Japan Volleyball Association dates itself to 1946.)
- **1922** [high] The first United States national championship is played at the Brooklyn Central Y.M.C.A. on 28–29 April 1922, run by the Central Volley Ball League: 23 teams from eleven states (Michigan, New Jersey, New York, Connecticut, Ohio, Indiana, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Colorado, Massachusetts) plus entries from the Dominion of Canada, playing 89 games. Pittsburgh Central Y.M.C.A. won the final round-robin on percentage (7–2, .778); Germantown, Pennsylvania's 'Big Red Team' was runner-up and Johnstown, New York third. Note: many secondary sources say '27 teams from 11 states' — the Volleyball Hall of Fame's own account says 23.
- **1922** [medium] FIVB's history states that the first national volleyball federations were founded in Czechoslovakia and Bulgaria in 1922. Czech scholarship instead dates the ČVaBS (Czechoslovak Volleyball and Basketball Federation) to 1924, with the game first officially introduced to Czechoslovakia in 1919 by American YMCA secretaries.
- **1923** [low] The clockwise rotation rule is introduced, obliging every player to serve in turn and to alternate between front and back row — replacing the previously ad hoc serving order and completing the structural logic of the six-player game.
- **1923** [medium] The first recorded official volleyball match in Russia is played on 28 July 1923 on Myasnitskaya Street in Moscow, between the Higher Art Theatre Workshops and the State Technical School of Cinematography.
- **1924** [low] Ira 'Irl' Tubbs of Galva, Illinois patents the needle-inflation valve for sports balls, ending the era in which a ball had to be unlaced and its bladder pulled out to be inflated. Laced volleyballs persisted for years afterward, but this is the technical hinge between the laced ball and the modern one.
- **1928** [high] The United States Volleyball Association (USVBA, now USA Volleyball) is formed to run tournaments and rules beyond the YMCA, and stages the first U.S. Open, open to non-YMCA teams.

### Questoes em aberto

- EXACT INVENTION DATE. '9 February 1895' is repeated by MassMoments, the New World Encyclopedia and most of the internet, but the International Volleyball Hall of Fame's own research says the date has no verifiable citation, that Morgan did not begin at Holyoke until 30 August 1895, and that the game was created 'in the winter' — i.e. December 1895. RECOMMENDATION: plaques should read '1895', with the February-vs-December dispute as a museum label of its own. Needs the Springfield College William G. Morgan Papers (MS 511) to settle.
- PANEL COUNT OF THE ORIGINAL BALL. Sources give 8 panels (volleyballhero.com), 12 (vintage-ball dealers) and 18 (sportsrec/secondary). None of these is a catalogued, dated artefact. Before finalising the hero ball asset, get a photograph of a provenanced pre-1920 Spalding volleyball from the IVHF or the Springfield College collection.
- WHEN THE FIRST PURPOSE-BUILT SPALDING BALL ACTUALLY APPEARED — 1896 or 1900. Wikipedia explicitly flags this as disputed. The 1897 handbook already specifies 25–27 in / 9–12 oz, which argues for a ball existing by 1897, but that clause may describe any leather-or-canvas ball rather than a Spalding product.
- CUBA 1905 vs 1906. Cuban sources give 17 October 1905, L. M. Ward, Havana YMCA, with a tournament on 28 October 1905; FIVB's official history says 1906; and a rival Cuban tradition credits U.S. Army officer August York. Spanish-language Cuban federation or EcuRed primary material would resolve it.
- THREE-HIT RULE: 1920 or 1922? Four independent American sources say 1920 (alongside the back-row attack rule); FIVB's own history page says the maximum of three contacts was established in 1922. Both cannot be plaque text.
- WHO REALLY FOUNDED THE FIRST NATIONAL FEDERATION. FIVB says Czechoslovakia and Bulgaria in 1922; Czech scholarship says the ČVaBS was founded in 1924; Wikipedia says the Great Japan Volleyball Association was established in 1921; Encyclopedia.com calls the USVBA of 1928 'the world's first national governing body'. All four claims conflict. Needs the FIVB centenary/statutes archive.
- THE 1922 BROOKLYN FIELD SIZE. The Volleyball Hall of Fame's own account says 23 teams and 89 games; FIVB-derived sources say 27 teams from 11 states. Prefer the IVHF number (23) on the plaque and footnote the discrepancy — this is exactly the kind of detail a museum gets caught on.
- ROTATION RULE, 1923. Sourced only to a paywalled SportsEdTV article. When clockwise rotation became mandatory — and whether it arrived with the six-player rule of 1918 or separately — is currently unverified. Do not put 1923 on a plaque without a second source.
- NET RAISED TO 7 ft 6 in IN 1900, AND COURT ENLARGED TO 35 × 60 ft IN 1912. Both appear only in secondary timelines (NCVA, Volleyball.com). The 1912 entry also duplicates the six-player change that FIVB dates to 1918, which suggests the timeline is compiled rather than sourced.
- CHINA: 1910 or 1911? FIVB's beach-history page says 1911; other sources say 1910 (Max Exner and J. Howard Crocker) and one says 1905 in schools.
- 'DR. FRANK WOOD' vs 'DR. FRANK WOODS'. FIVB and the IVHF spell it differently on the same fact. Check the 1916–17 Spalding Volley Ball Guide, which is the source both are citing.
- WHAT THE HOLYOKE PLAYERS ACTUALLY WORE IN 1895 AND AT SPRINGFIELD ON 7 JULY 1896. The uniform reconstruction above is inferred from the 1901 Spalding catalogue and general YMCA physical-culture dress. The Springfield College archive holds photographs from this era; obtaining one would let the character models be sourced rather than inferred.
- THE HOLYOKE YMCA GYMNASIUM INTERIOR. The building at High and Appleton opened in 1886 and burned in 1943, and no architectural description, architect attribution or interior dimension was found in any accessible source. The reconstruction above is typological. Holyoke Public Library local history, the Holyoke Canal tour archive (currently blocked to automated fetch) and Sanborn fire-insurance maps are the paths to a faithful model.
- EARLY WOMEN'S PLAY. One fetched source records an original-rules provision that 'to protect the fingers of the ladies, they were allowed to catch the ball and then throw it back into play', and the 1916 Cubbon survey counts 50,000 YWCA players — a very large share. The women's side of the origins story is under-documented here and deserves its own research pass before the room is written.

### Correcoes do fact-check (24 | 9 marcos confirmados)

Morgan, Japan Volleyball Association, Inter-Allied Games, 1913 Far Eastern Championship Games, Elwood Brown, Hyozo Omori, Irl Tubbs, Faustball, Holyoke, Chicopee; FIVB's official history; the International Volleyball Hall of Fame history page; NCVA; Encyclopedia.com; New World Encyclopedia; Springfield College; Google Patents). Note: the WebSearch budget for this session was already exhausted at the outset, so all verification was done by direct WebFetch against known URLs; several targets (volleyhall.org championship page, usavolleyball.org, massmoments, holyokelibrary.org, verbatim 1897 rule text) returned 404/403 and could not be reached, which itself is a finding for the claims resting on them.

POSITIVELY CONFIRMED (9): the IVHF invention-date dispute, including Morgan's 30 August 1895 arrival at Holyoke and the December 1895 dating; Elwood S. Brown and the Philippines in 1910; the set-and-spike emerging in the Philippines in 1916; the 21-to-15-point change in 1917; six players a side in 1918; the three-hit and back-row-attack rules in 1920 (with FIVB's 1922 dissent correctly disclosed); the Great Japan Volleyball Association in 1921 and the modern JVA's 1946 date, both verbatim; the first Russian match on 28 July 1923 on Myasnitskaya Street between the Higher Art Theatre Workshops and the State Technical School of Cinematography, exactly as stated; and the USVBA in 1928, which Encyclopedia.com does indeed call the world's first national governing body. Morgan's birth and death dates, the 6 ft 6 in net, the 25 × 50 ft court, the 7 July 1896 conference with Curran and Lynch as captains, and Spalding's Chicopee Falls works also check out as sub-claims inside entries that failed on other grounds.

MOST SERIOUS PROBLEMS: (1) The 1924 needle-valve milestone is wrong in nearly every particular — Irl Tubbs was a football coach born in Pulaski County, Kentucky, not Galva, Illinois, his documented invention is an inverted air valve for a football, and no patent exists in Google Patents; this false claim propagates into two equipment descriptions. ## 1930s–1940s

**This is the twenty years in which volleyball stopped being an American YMCA recreation and became a governed international sport: Eastern Europe and the USSR turned it into a mass competitive game, World War II froze and then scattered it, and on 20 April 1947 fourteen federations met in Paris to found the FIVB and sign one rulebook for the whole world.**

### Paleta visual

TWO ROOMS IN ONE ERA, and the split should be visible. THE 1930s HALF — optimistic late Art Deco. Palette: cream and bone (#E8DFCB), warm oak and amber varnish (#B07C3F), oxblood (#6E1F2A), forest green (#25402F), muted teal, brass and gold leaf (#C9A227), with a hard Soviet red (#C0272D) reserved for the USSR case. Materials: sprung honey maple flooring with hand-painted 50 mm white lines, varnished wood, chromed tube, black Bakelite, unbleached cotton canvas, tarred hemp cord, tanned cowhide, cast iron radiators, vitreous enamel, plaster mouldings, wire-glass steel windows. THE 1940s HALF — austerity then reconstruction. Palette drops saturation and warms toward grey-brown: khaki, oxidised olive, gunmetal, kraft paper (#B9A17E), whitewash over brick, newsprint grey; single-colour stencil signage; scarcity textures (mended canvas, re-nailed boards, painted-over older lettering ghosting through). Then, for the 1947 Paris congress alcove, the palette flips to Second Empire opulence: gilt (#C6A664), deep green baize, crimson damask, pier-glass mirrors, white-and-gold plasterwork, parquet, crystal. LIGHTING: everything is tungsten and daylight, never cool. Interiors key at 2700–3000K from caged industrial pendants or opal glass globes, with long dusty shafts of 5000K daylight from tall multi-pane windows; the Paris salon adds a chandelier at 2400K with strong specular sparkle on gilt and glass. Practical light sources are few and warm; shadows are deep and slightly green-brown, never blue. Volumetric dust in every beam. TYPOGRAPHY: Futura (1927) and Gill Sans (1928) for neutral text; Peignot (Cassandre, 1937) — a stressed, unicase Art Deco sans, popular through the late 1940s — for anything French or ceremonial; Kabel and Bifur for display; heavy condensed grotesques and slab serifs for Cyrillic Constructivist panels; stencil faces for wartime material; Century Schoolbook / Cheltenham for printed rulebook body text. ARCHITECTURE: a YMCA/municipal gymnasium spine — exposed steel truss roof, brick or tiled dado walls, tall wire-glass steel casements, folding wooden bleachers, iron radiators, a wall clock with black Roman-ish numerals — opening into two contrasting alcoves: a Constructivist red-and-black Soviet bay with photomontage banners and enamel badge cases, and a gilded Second Empire Parisian salon with a green baize table, fourteen nameplates and a domed ceiling. ### Objetos de acervo (9)

#### Laced 18-panel leather indoor volleyball, c. 1935–1948

Sphere, 65–67 cm circumference — model at 21.0 cm diameter, 260–280 g. Cover is full-grain tanned cowhide in warm natural tan (#C89A63) to mid-saddle brown (#8B5A2B); some tournament balls are bleached off-white/bone (#E8DFCB). Panel layout is the classic 18-panel arrangement: SIX groups of THREE parallel rectangular strips, the six groups oriented on the three axes of a cube (two opposing groups per axis, each rotated 90° from its neighbours). Each flattened panel is a rounded-corner rectangle roughly 11 cm × 3.6 cm with a 5 mm radius. Panels are hand-sewn inside-out and turned, so seams read as RECESSED grooves ~2 mm deep and ~2 mm wide, not raised welts — this is the single most important detail for the shader. Waxed linen thread in a slightly darker tone, visible as a fine dotted stitch line 3 mm inside each panel edge. Closure: a 9–10 cm slit on one panel, edged with 8–12 punched brass or plain leather eyelets and closed with a flat rawhide lace crossed 4–6 times, sitting slightly proud of the surface (this is the seam that stings a forearm pass — the whole reason laceless valve balls replaced it mid-century). Interior: latex rubber bladder (bicycle-inner-tube derived) wrapped in a cheesecloth/canvas liner; on the laceless variant, replace lace with a flush black rubber needle valve, 8 mm, set in one panel. Finish: matte to low satin, NOT glossy. Wear: darkened, polished, slightly greasy sheen around the equator; scuffed pale abrasion on the two 'polar' panel groups; grit in the seam grooves. Optional maker's mark: a small gold or black foil oval stamp on one panel reading OFFICIAL VOLLEY BALL / SPALDING, 35 × 18 mm, partly rubbed away.

*Por que importa:* This is the ball of the entire era and the object a player will pick up first. The 18-panel/6-groups-of-3 layout survived unchanged from 1900 until Mikasa's 8-panel ball in 2008, so getting the panel topology right instantly reads as 'pre-modern volleyball'. The rawhide lace is the era's signature — it is why the 1930s dig looks nothing like a modern one.

#### Tarred-cord net, canvas tape and guyed posts, c. 1940s

Net: rectangular curtain approx 9.5 m long × 1.0 m deep. Square mesh of ~10 cm openings, knotted from waxed/tarred cotton or hemp twine 2–3 mm thick, colour dark chocolate to near-black (#2A211A), fibrous and slightly fuzzy — never the crisp black nylon of today. It sags visibly between posts and is never perfectly planar. Top band: a doubled strip of natural cotton canvas 5 cm wide, unbleached cream (#DED3B8), machine-stitched along both edges, with either a hemp rope or a thin galvanised steel cable threaded through it; tensioned with a wooden toggle or a screw turnbuckle at one post. Bottom edge: usually a single cord only, or a narrower 3 cm tape — the second bottom tape is a mid-century addition. NO side bands, NO antennae (antennae are a 1970s innovation — including them is an anachronism bug). Posts: either turned wooden poles ~90 mm diameter, varnished or painted dark green/grey, or 60 mm galvanised steel pipe, standing 2.55 m so the net crosses at 2.43 m (men) / 2.24 m (women). Posts sit in cast-iron floor sockets or heavy cross-braced wooden feet, and are additionally guyed with two hemp ropes each running out at ~45° to floor rings or wall hooks — the guy ropes are visually essential and are the trip hazard of the period.

*Por que importa:* The net is the room's spatial anchor and the object that dates the scene fastest. Antennae, side bands, or a clean synthetic mesh will read as 1990s. The guy ropes and sagging tarred cord say 1940s gymnasium immediately.

#### Men's national-team kit, 1948 Rome / 1949 Prague

Top: cotton jersey knit, either a sleeveless singlet with 3 cm shoulder straps or a short-sleeve crew tee; slightly loose, no stretch, drapes and creases like a heavy T-shirt. Colour blocks are flat and saturated: white (#F2F0E8), cream, navy (#1E2A44), maroon (#6E1F2A), kelly green, or Czechoslovak/Soviet red (#B4232A). A common device is a single horizontal chest band 6–8 cm deep in the contrast colour. Numbers: felt or chenille appliqué, or coarse screen print, 18–20 cm tall, heavy block sans-serif, BACK ONLY on most kits (chest numbers become mandatory later). Small woven or embroidered club/national crest, ~55 mm, on the left chest; national teams carry a shield or Cyrillic 'СССР'. NO sponsor logos, NO manufacturer branding — anachronism risk. Shorts: high-waisted, hem at mid-thigh with a 3–5 cm inseam, cut in cotton twill or satin-finish rayon; elastic waistband with an external drawstring or a fabric belt through two loops; contrasting 2 cm side stripe running from waistband to hem. Socks: heavy ribbed cotton, calf-length, white with two horizontal stripes at the cuff, worn slouched. Shoes: low-cut cotton canvas upper (white, black or oxblood), 5–6 metal eyelets, round rubber toe cap, gum or black vulcanised thermoplastic-rubber sole with a shallow herringbone or plain-suction tread, foxing tape around the perimeter — the volleyball-specific canvas shoe dates from the late 1930s. Knee pads: optional and bulky — thick wool or felt padding faced with leather, held on with elastic sleeves or two buckled straps, standing 20–25 mm proud of the leg with a stiff domed 'turtle-shell' profile, in undyed grey-brown. Materials note for the artist: everything is matte, absorbent, and wrinkles hard. Nothing here is spandex, nothing is fitted, nothing is shiny.

*Por que importa:* A mannequin in this kit is the visitor's fastest read of 'how different the athletes were'. The high-waisted shorts, back-only numbers and canvas shoes are the period tells, and the absence of any commercial branding is the quiet story of an amateur, state- and club-funded sport.

#### Soviet club / national kit and enamel sports badges, 1930s–1940s

Kit as above but with distinct signatures: a heavier wool-blend or brushed cotton jersey; the sports-society name set large across the chest in Cyrillic — «ДИНАМО», «СПАРТАК», «ЛОКОМОТИВ» — in a condensed heavy sans or a bold slab, letters 70–90 mm tall, applied in felt appliqué. Palette is red (#C0272D) and white, or Dinamo blue-white, or Spartak red with a broad white diagonal sash across the chest. National team: white jersey, «СССР» in red across the chest. Accompanying objects for a display case: circular and shield-shaped enamel-on-brass sports badges, 20–30 mm across, screw-back or pin-back, in cloisonné-style hard enamel — red star, hammer-and-sickle, wheat ears, the GTO ('Ready for Labour and Defence') badge in red and gold; a stamped-brass 'Мастер спорта' style badge; a small silvered championship medal, 32 mm, on a red-white ribbon. Metal finishes are soft brass and dull nickel, with glossy vitreous enamel that chips at the rim.

*Por que importa:* The USSR did not just adopt volleyball, it industrialised it — 400,000 players by 1933, a state championship from 1933, and world and European titles within a year of joining the FIVB. The badge case is the single best object for telling that story without text.

#### The FIVB founding documents and the Paris congress table, 18–20 April 1947

Setting: a salon of the Grand Hôtel, 2 rue Scribe, Paris — Second Empire, opened 5 May 1862, all gilding, arcaded plasterwork, tall pier mirrors, candelabra sconces, parquet under a large patterned carpet, and (in the grandest room, the Salon Opéra) a 14-metre-high domed ceiling with a monumental chandelier. Furniture: one long table, 6–7 m, covered in dark green baize with a 25 mm gilt-braid edge; bentwood or carved-back chairs; a lectern for the chair. Table dressing: folded white card nameplates, 200 × 70 mm, hand-lettered in black ink with the nation names IN FRENCH — BELGIQUE, BRÉSIL, TCHÉCOSLOVAQUIE, ÉGYPTE, FRANCE, HONGRIE, ITALIE, PAYS-BAS, POLOGNE, PORTUGAL, ROUMANIE, URUGUAY, ÉTATS-UNIS D'AMÉRIQUE, YOUGOSLAVIE. Documents: typed statutes on foolscap/A4 onion-skin paper, cream-yellowed, French language, struck on a mechanical typewriter with a purple-black ribbon so the type is uneven and slightly embossed on the reverse; the title page reads FÉDÉRATION INTERNATIONALE DE VOLLEY-BALL — STATUTS (note the hyphen; it is the period spelling), with the year in Roman or plain figures. Carbon copies on thinner blue-tinted flimsy sit in a second stack. Signatures in blue-black fountain-pen ink, 14 of them, with an embossed dry seal or a red wax/paper wafer seal at the foot. Props: Gestetner-duplicated agenda sheets with the violet spirit-duplicator tint, a black Bakelite ashtray, a cut-glass water carafe and tumblers, a marbled Waterman or Parker fountain pen, a leather blotter, a brass bell. Lighting: warm tungsten from the chandelier plus tall windows with sheer curtains — a 2700K key with strong specular hits on gilt and mirror.

*Por que importa:* This table IS the decade. Everything modern about the sport — one rulebook, one federation, world championships, eventually the Olympics — is downstream of what was signed here over three days in April 1947. It is also the room's emotional payload: a French, Czech and Polish handshake in a bombed-out continent two years after the war.

#### USVBA Annual Official Volleyball Rules and Reference Guide, late 1930s–1940s edition

Saddle-stitched or perfect-bound paperback booklet, approx 140 × 210 mm, 100–160 pages, 12–15 mm thick. Cover: coated card in a single flat spot colour — oxblood, forest green, or navy — with the title reverse-knocked-out in white or overprinted in black, set in a condensed grotesque or a Deco-influenced face; a small line-art vignette of a player or a court plan; the year in large figures. Interior: uncoated cream newsprint-grade stock, single-column body in 9 pt serif (Century Schoolbook / Cheltenham family), rule numbers in bold, black-and-white line diagrams of the court with dimensions called out in FEET AND INCHES (30 × 60 ft court, 8 ft net) — a deliberate detail, since the metric international court arrives with the FIVB. Halftone photographs at coarse 65–85 lpi screen, grey and soft. Back pages carry period advertisements for Spalding, Voit and Wilson equipment with engraved-style product illustrations. Physical state for the exhibit: sun-faded spine, dog-eared corners, a rubber-stamped YMCA or club ownership mark on the cover, pencil annotations in the margins. Alongside it, place a printed FIVB rules booklet from 1947 or later in French, thinner, with metric court figures — the two side by side ARE the standardisation story.

*Por que importa:* This is the physical embodiment of governance, and the source that modern rule histories still cite (the 1970 edition carried William T. Odeneal's 'A Summary of Seventy-Five Years of Rules'). Two booklets on one plinth — imperial American, metric international — explain the whole 1947 unification without a word of caption.

#### Wooden referee stand, manual scoreboard and gymnasium fittings, c. 1940s

*Por que importa:* Gives the room its architecture and scale, and carries three separate rule-history details (the painted position crosses from 1935, imperial line spacing, a referee elevated but unprotected) that reward a player who looks closely.

#### 1948 Rome and 1949 Prague award medals and championship trophy

Medals: struck bronze and silvered bronze discs, 45–55 mm diameter, 3–4 mm thick, with a low-relief obverse — a stylised classicised athlete mid-spike, or a she-wolf/SPQR motif for Rome and a Prague/Czechoslovak lion for the 1949 events — and a plain or laurel-wreathed reverse with incised lettering: CHAMPIONNAT D'EUROPE / ROMA MCMXLVIII, or MISTROVSTVÍ SVĚTA / PRAHA 1949. *Por que importa:* The first continental and world titles gave the new federation something to hand over, and both went east — Czechoslovakia in Rome 1948, the USSR in Prague 1949. The silverware is how the room shows the balance of power shifting to the Eastern bloc.

### Marcos (33)

- **1928** [high] The United States Volleyball Association (USVBA, now USA Volleyball) is formed at the annual meeting of the Joint Volley Ball Rules Committee — described by Encyclopedia.com as the world's first national governing body for the sport. The first US Open is staged the same year, opening competition to non-YMCA squads. It ran on unpaid volunteer labour, largely YMCA personnel, for its first ~45 years.
- **1928** [high] The Polish Volleyball Federation (Polski Związek Piłki Siatkowej, PZPS) is founded — one of the oldest national volleyball bodies in Europe. It joins the FIVB in 1947 as a founding member.
- **1929** [medium] Cuba organises the first men's tournament played under 'American' rules at the Caribbean and Central American Games — an early sign of the rules-dialect problem the FIVB would later have to solve.
- **1932** [high] US rules change: the centre line is extended indefinitely, players may go outside their own court to play the ball, but may not interchange positions or move outside their understood playing area. (From 'A Summary of Seventy-Five Years of Rules' by William T. Odeneal, originally published in the 1970 USVBA Annual Official Volleyball Rules and Reference Guide.)
- **4 April 1933** [high] The first USSR volleyball championship opens at the Palace of Metalworkers in Dnepropetrovsk, contested by the representative teams of five cities. The first four editions (1933–1936) were officially called 'All-Union Volleyball Holidays'. Moscow won every one of them, in both the men's and women's events; the women's first edition was held in Moscow.
- **1933** [medium] Contemporary accounts put over 400,000 registered players already active in the Soviet Union at the time of the first national championship — volleyball had become a mass participation sport in the USSR years before it was one anywhere in Western Europe.
- **1934** [high] The first attempt to create an independent international volleyball federation is made at the congress of the International Amateur Handball Federation (IAHF) in Stockholm — and fails. Volleyball remained an appendage of the handball federation until after WWII.
- **1934** [medium] In the United States, national volleyball referees are formally approved and recognised for the first time.
- **1935** [high] US rules change: two crosses are painted on each side of the court to define the six player positions; screening the server becomes a foul; any touching of the net becomes a foul; and the 'continuation' rule is introduced (a play is not complete until the player resumes normal control on the floor).
- **2 February 1936** [high] The Fédération Française de Volley-Ball (FFVB) is created when Félix Castellant deposits its statutes with the French Ministry of Leisure. It was founded on the initiative of three former members of the French table tennis federation. This is the federation that would host and drive the FIVB's founding eleven years later.
- **1936** [medium] At the Berlin Olympic Games, Poland proposes forming a committee to establish an international volleyball federation, reportedly comprising 13 European federations, 5 from the Americas and 3 from Asia. The initiative does not produce a federation before the war.
- **1937** [high] At the Amateur Athletic Union convention in Boston, action is taken to recognise the USVBA as the official national governing body for volleyball in the United States.
- **1938** [high] Blocking enters the written rules for the first time: one- or two-person blocks are permitted provided the blockers occupy adjacent positions. Blocking is defined as impeding the ball at the net.
- **13 August 1938** [high] The first French national men's championship is played at Pontaillac, near Royan, with only four Parisian clubs entered; Amicale de Paris wins. France also plays its first international match in 1938, at the Stade Pierre-de-Coubertin in Paris, losing to Greece. The first French women's championship follows in 1941, won by Villa Primerose de Bordeaux.
- **1938** [high] The Soviet championship converts from city representative teams to club teams representing the voluntary sports societies (DSO) and government departments — the Dinamo / Spartak / Lokomotiv structure that would define Soviet volleyball for fifty years.
- **1941–1944** [high] No USSR championships are contested — the Soviet national competition is suspended for four consecutive years by the war. It resumes in 1945, with Dynamo Moscow taking four straight men's titles (1945–1948) and Lokomotiv Moscow dominating the women's event.
- **1942** [high] Rules change: the ball may be played with any part of the body from the knees upward (previously it had to be played above the waist).
- **27 December 1942** [high] William G. Morgan, the inventor of the game, dies in Lockport, New York, aged 72 (born 23 January 1870). WARNING FOR PLAQUE COPY: several widely-copied volleyball timelines, including NCVA's, state '1940 ... dies at the age of 68'. That is wrong — Wikipedia and the International Volleyball Hall of Fame both give 27 December 1942.
- **1943–1945** [medium] The USVBA cancels its national championships in 1944 and 1945 — the only interruption in the series since 1928. Meanwhile the game is played wherever people are confined or deployed: Ansel Adams photographed Japanese American internees playing volleyball at the Manzanar Relocation Center around 1943, and the YMCA War Office put volleyball into the recreation programme for US forces at home and abroad.
- **26 August 1946** [high] In Prague, on the occasion of a friendly match between the Czechoslovak and French national teams, representatives of the Czechoslovak, French and Polish federations sign what is regarded as the first official document of the future FIVB: it creates a commission to organise the International Federation, calls for a constitutive congress, and resolves to launch a European or World Championship at an early date. Paul Libaud's own Hall of Fame biography places a related meeting with Polish and Czech representatives at the Graf Coffee House in Prague.
- **18–20 April 1947** [high] The Constitutive Congress of the Fédération Internationale de Volley-Ball meets in Paris, at the Grand Hôtel (2 rue Scribe, beside the Opéra Garnier). Fourteen national federations representing five continents attend; the FIVB is officially formed, with FIVB's own anniversary material giving 20 April 1947 as the founding date. Paris is chosen as the headquarters.
- **18–20 April 1947** [high] The 14 founding nations are: Belgium, Brazil, Czechoslovakia, Egypt, France, Hungary, Italy, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Uruguay, the United States of America and Yugoslavia. Note the era spelling used on the documents: 'Volley-Ball', hyphenated.
- **1947** [high] Paul Libaud of France — president of the FFVB from 1945 — is elected the FIVB's first president. He holds the office for 37 years, until 1984, when Rubén Acosta succeeds him and moves the headquarters from Paris to Lausanne.
- **1947** [high] The Paris Congress approves the first international rulebook, unifying the divergent European and American codes into a single set of playing rules. That same year the US rules add: only front-line players may interchange positions to form a two-person block.
- **1947–1952** [medium] COURT AND NET — handle with care. Under American rules the court had been 30 × 60 ft since 1912 and the net 8 ft (2.438 m) since 1916; a 1923 international standard of 18.28 × 9.14 m is simply that court in metric. The modern 18 × 9 m court with nets at 2.43 m (men) and 2.24 m (women) — very slightly SMALLER and LOWER than the old imperial figures — comes out of the 1947 unification, but sources disagree on whether it was fixed at the 1947 Congress itself or settled by 1952. Do not put a single hard year on a plaque without an archival rulebook.
- **1948** [high] Major rules clarification and rewrite: players must be in their own areas before the ball leaves the server's hands, blocking gets a better definition, and service is limited to a small portion of the back court.
- **24–26 September 1948** [high] The first Men's European Championship is played in Rome, at the Stadio Nicola Pietrangeli, over three days. Six teams — Belgium, Czechoslovakia, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal — play a single round robin, 15 matches. Czechoslovakia wins all five matches without dropping a set; France take silver, hosts Italy bronze. Czechoslovakia was the only Eastern European entrant.
- **1948** [high] The USSR Volleyball Federation joins the FIVB, one year after its foundation — and immediately becomes the dominant force in the sport.
- **1948** [high] In Los Angeles, the first beach volleyball tournament to offer a prize is held; the winning two-man team receives a case of Pepsi. The two-man beach format itself dates from summer 1930, when Paul 'Pablo' Johnson ran a two-a-side game at the Santa Monica Athletic Club because only four players turned up.
- **1949** [high] Rules change: timed games (8 minutes of ball in play) are permitted, teams must win by two points, and three-person blocks become legal provided all three are front-row players.
- **10–18 September 1949** [high] The first FIVB Volleyball Men's World Championship is held in Prague, Czechoslovakia — ten teams (Czechoslovakia, France, USSR, Poland, Bulgaria, Romania, Netherlands, Italy, Hungary, Belgium); Uruguay and Yugoslavia declined to take part. The Soviet Union win it undefeated (8–0, 24–2 in sets), Czechoslovakia take silver, Bulgaria bronze. The tournament was opened by Czechoslovak president Klement Gottwald — the Cold War arrives in volleyball at its very first world championship.
- **10–18 September 1949** [high] The first Women's European Championship runs in Prague alongside the men's world event: seven teams (Czechoslovakia, France, Hungary, Netherlands, Poland, Romania, USSR), single round robin. The Soviet Union win 6–0, Czechoslovakia silver, Poland bronze. The USVBA also starts its women's division in 1949.
- **1946** [medium] National federations reconstitute across post-war Europe and immediately feed the FIVB: the Italian federation FIPAV is founded 31 March 1946 and the Volleyball Federation of Yugoslavia in 1946, both becoming FIVB founding members in 1947. The IAHF, volleyball's reluctant former parent body, ceases to exist in 1946.

### Questoes em aberto

- Which specific salon inside the Grand Hôtel (2 rue Scribe) hosted the 18–20 April 1947 Constitutive Congress? The Salon Opéra is the hotel's famous room, but nothing I found confirms the congress used it rather than a smaller meeting salon. Do not caption the space by name without archival confirmation.
- Was the FIVB constituted on 18 or on 20 April 1947? The congress ran 18–20 April; FIVB's own anniversary material gives 20 April 1947 as the founding date, and Wikipedia says the entity was formed 'between 18 and 20 April'. A plaque should say either '20 April 1947' or 'the congress of 18–20 April 1947', not both.
- Were the 18 × 9 m court and the 2.43 m / 2.24 m net heights actually adopted at the April 1947 Congress, or phased in and finalised by 1952? Secondary sources split on this and I could not obtain a scan of the 1947 or 1949 FIVB rulebook. This is the single highest-risk fact for a wrong date on a plaque.
- No scan, photograph, or transcript of the 1947 FIVB statutes could be located online. The physical form described (typed onion-skin, French, wafer seal, 14 signatures) is period-plausible reconstruction, not a documented replica.
- The design of the 1949 Prague World Championship trophy and the 1948 Rome European Championship medals is unverified. Wikipedia shows a trophy image but no descriptive text; treat any modelled award as generic period silverware.
- Which hall in Prague hosted the 1949 World Championship? The Wikipedia article lists one venue in one city but does not name it.
- Was the ball used at the 1949 World Championship laced or laceless, and who manufactured it — a Czechoslovak maker, or an imported Spalding/Voit? Not established. Similarly unresolved: whether Soviet 1930s championship balls were domestically produced or imported.
- The 1936 Berlin detail (Poland proposing a committee of 13 European, 5 American and 3 Asian federations) rests on a single source, the Italian Wikipedia history of volleyball. Corroborate before putting the numbers on a wall.
- Widely-copied timelines (NCVA among them) place William G. Morgan's death in 1940 at age 68. Wikipedia and the International Volleyball Hall of Fame both give 27 December 1942, Lockport NY, aged 72. I have used 1942, but flagging it because the wrong version is very common in volleyball sources and may appear in whatever reference the art team pulls.
- NCVA's timeline also produced, in one summarisation pass, a claim that the official ball became 'a twelve-piece laceless leather-covered ball' by 1940. A verbatim re-read of the same page showed no such entry, and every other source describes 18 panels until 2008. Treat the twelve-panel claim as unsupported and do not model it.
- Exact panel dimensions, lace length and eyelet count on a period ball are extrapolated from the 65–67 cm circumference and the standard 18-panel topology, not measured from a surviving artefact. If precision matters, the International Volleyball Hall of Fame archive in Holyoke (Edward DeGroot Collection, which holds the 1st FIVB World Congress photograph) is the place to ask.
- Founding dates for the Czechoslovak volleyball federation and for the Soviet 'All-Union Volleyball Section' (reportedly 1932, within the All-Union Council of Physical Culture) are not confirmed on a source I was able to fetch directly.
- Whether the FFVB itself, or the FIVB, kept a signed original of the 26 August 1946 Prague document — described as the first official document of the future FIVB — and whether it survives.

## 1950s–1960s: The Cold War Court and Volleyball's Olympic Debut

**In two decades volleyball went from a Soviet-dominated Eastern-bloc championship sport to an Olympic medal event, and on 23 October 1964 twelve Japanese textile-mill workers beat the USSR in Tokyo in front of two-thirds of their nation — while 9,000 km east, Californians were quietly inventing the two-man beach game that would become the sport's other half.**

### Paleta visual

Colour: Soviet flag red #CE1126 and oxblood #7B1E22 against board-formed concrete grey #9A9A94, warm parquet and pale strip hardwood #C9A87C, black net mesh, off-white canvas #EFE9DA, brass and gold-anodised trim. Hinomaru scarlet #BC002D on off-white for the Japanese half. #### Tokyo 1964 Mikasa 18-panel leather match ball

A pure sphere, 20.7–21.3 cm in diameter (FIVB circumference 65–67 cm), 260–280 g, inflated to 0.30–0.325 kg/cm² so it reads as firm but not drum-tight — model a very slight flattening under contact, not a perfect ball. Seams are RECESSED channels 2–3 mm deep and about 3 mm wide, with visible waxed-thread hand stitching running down the trough; the panels bulge gently between the seams, giving the ball a slightly quilted silhouette rather than a smooth one. Surface: unbleached-to-ivory white leather (roughly #F4F1E8 to #EAE4D6), matte-to-satin with a fine pebbled grain; add grime darkening in the seam channels and a faint grey scuff band where the ball is hit. A small circular valve patch (~15 mm) sits centred on one panel. NO colour blocking, NO panel dyes: coloured competition balls did not arrive until 1998, so any blue/yellow ball in a 1964 scene is a bug. Mikasa supplied the balls for volleyball's Olympic debut in 1964.

*Por que importa:* This is the hero prop of the whole room. Volleyball's Olympic debut ball is a Japanese-made object, and the plain white 18-panel leather sphere is the single most era-defining silhouette of the period — instantly wrong if you substitute a modern tri-colour 8-panel ball.

#### 1964-spec net, posts and court — the 'no-antennae' net assembly

Net: 1.00 m tall, 9.50–10.00 m long, of black 10 cm square knotted mesh. Top band 7 cm wide, two-fold WHITE canvas, with a steel tension cable threaded through it (the cable is what makes the top edge straight and the mesh sag slightly in the bays between). Bottom band 5 cm, same white canvas, with a tensioning rope. Two vertical white side bands, 5 cm wide × 1 m tall, sit directly above each sideline. CRITICAL PERIOD DETAIL: NO ANTENNAE — the fibreglass red/white antennae were only recommended from 1968 and standardised in the 1970s, so a 1964 net terminates in bare white side bands. Net height 2.43 m (men) / 2.24 m (women), measured at centre; period nets sag more than modern ones, so model 2–4 cm of droop mid-span. Posts: round or square uprights standing about 2.55 m, set 0.5–1.0 m outside each sideline, in floor sockets; period indoor posts were commonly painted steel with a rack-and-pinion or ratchet tensioner at the base and often external guy wires or braces (modern rules forbid wires — for 1964, wires are correct flavour, mark as inferred). Court: 18 × 9 m of pale sprung hardwood strip flooring, lines painted 5 cm wide in white (or dark green/black on a light floor); attack lines 3 m either side of the centre line; a wooden referee stand — a stepped platform with a handrail — clamped to one post so the first referee's eyes sit roughly 50 cm above the net tape. No libero, no rally scoring: matches were best-of-five sets to 15 by side-out.

*Por que importa:* The absent antennae and the sagging canvas-banded net are the fastest way for a visitor to feel 'this is 1964, not now'. It also frames the room's central diorama and defines the scale of everything else.

#### Japan women's 'NK' match uniform (Nichibo Kaizuka pattern), Tokyo 1964

The competition kit was developed for the Nichibo Kaizuka squad and is documented as the 'NK-style' uniform. Top: short-sleeved, close-fitting jersey in a polyester/cotton blend (chosen for durability plus moisture absorption and sweat-wicking), with a narrow ribbed collar and ribbed cuffs knitted in woolen nylon so they hold their shape and grip the arm — model these as a 2 cm ribbed band at the neck and a 3 cm band at each sleeve hem, slightly tighter than the sleeve body. THE SIGNATURE DETAIL: a metal back zipper running from the collar down the upper back, deliberately OFFSET to one side of the spine rather than centred, because a centre zipper dug into the players' backs during the rolling receive — and the offset distance was tailored individually player by player. That zipper is the physical fossil of the kaiten reshību and should be modelled visibly (a 12–18 cm zip tape, roughly 3–5 cm off centre). Bottom: high-waisted, mid-thigh athletic shorts/bloomers with an elasticated waist and a slightly bloused leg; before this kit existed the players trained in loose 'lantern' bloomers and plain cotton shirts, which is the right look for the factory-gym exhibit. Numbers 1–12 on the back in block sans-serif; national identification lettered NIPPON, not JAPAN. Colours: national red-and-white (hinomaru scarlet, approx. #BC002D, against off-white). CAUTION — I could not confirm from a written source whether the match jersey was white with red trim or red with white trim; check a colour photograph before locking the shader. Footwear: low-cut canvas-upper court shoes with flat gum-rubber soles, plus cloth knee protection.

*Por que importa:* It is a garment engineered around a single technique. The off-centre zipper is a museum-grade artefact: it proves, in stitching, how far Daimatsu's rolling receive reshaped the sport's equipment — and it's the sort of detail a player would notice and remember.

#### Soviet Union 'CCCP' match kit, 1949–1968

The uniform of the era's dominant nation, worn across the 1949 Prague and 1952 Moscow world titles and both Tokyo 1964 tournaments. Short-sleeved crew-neck jersey in heavy combed cotton jersey knit with a slightly ribbed collar; the fabric is matte and holds creases (do not use a shiny modern polyester shader). Chest carries CCCP in Cyrillic block capitals, arched or straight, roughly 5–6 cm letter height, and often a small Soviet state emblem or hammer-and-sickle above it; back numbers in the same face. Colour scheme: Soviet flag red (approx. #CE1126) with white lettering is the period-typical treatment, with a white alternate carrying red lettering — mark the exact 1964 combination as unverified. Shorts: plain, mid-thigh, elasticated waist, either white or navy. Men's kit reads noticeably baggier and heavier than the Japanese kit; the Soviet women's team was also visibly taller than Japan's, which is worth building into the character proportions. Shoes: low canvas court shoes, gum sole. Optional prop: a red USSR tracksuit jacket with a full-length zip, white collar and cuff stripes, and CCCP across the back.

*Por que importa:* Volleyball in these two decades was, above everything, a Soviet sport: USSR men won the 1949, 1952, 1960 and 1962 world titles plus Olympic gold in 1964 and 1968, and USSR women won the 1952, 1956 and 1960 worlds. The CCCP jersey is the visual shorthand for the wall that Japan had to break through.

Single-breasted jacket in BRIGHT RED matte worsted wool — flat and chalky, not glossy — cut with a three-button front rolled to two (the top buttonhole disappears under the lapel roll), gold metal buttons, THREE PATCH POCKETS (one breast, two hip) and a single centre back vent. Worn over white trousers, a white shirt and, in period images, a tie; a delegation crest sits on the breast pocket (rings-plus-rising-sun style — treat the exact crest as unverified). Yasuyuki Mochizuki organised the tailoring and had been pushing for red blazers since Rome 1960; Kensuke Ishizu of VAN Jacket is often credited but was officially listed only for staff uniforms, and the historian W. David Marx concludes the outfit was chosen by committee rather than authored by one designer. Model the wool with visible weave at close range and a slightly stiff, structured drape — 1964 tailoring, not a soft modern blazer.

*Por que importa:* This is the outfit the Oriental Witches wore into the stadium, and the moment post-war Japan chose to present itself to the world in its own flag colours. It makes a superb standing mannequin exhibit next to the match kit — ceremonial red versus working red.

#### Komazawa Indoor Ball Sports Field — the arena shell

The main volleyball venue of the 1964 Games (Komazawa Olympic Park, Setagaya, Tokyo), completed March 1964 after a December 1962 groundbreaking, built by the Tokyo Metropolitan Olympics Facilities Construction Office. Reinforced concrete. Arena floor 1,629 m² at 35.4 m × 46 m. Seating capacity is recorded as 2,359 by the Japanese venue record and 3,908 in the Olympic listing (the higher figure presumably including temporary Olympic seating) — pick one and note it, but do not model a 10,000-seat bowl. Interior to build: a single rectangular hall, pale hardwood strip floor with painted court lines, banked bleacher seating close to the sidelines on the long sides, a low ceiling of exposed steel roof trusses with pendant industrial luminaires and a clerestory band of glazing near the roof line, exposed board-formed concrete on the end walls. Dress it with: manual flip-card scoreboards, folding wooden chairs for the officials' table, a wooden referee stand clamped to one net post, low advertising-free perimeter boards, an Olympic rings banner, wooden camera scaffolds for the NHK broadcast, and floor-level photographers on their knees. Lighting is warm tungsten (roughly 2800–3000 K) with hard shadows and visible falloff into the seating — nothing like modern LED flood. For the wider park, add the neighbouring Komazawa Gymnasium (Yoshinobu Ashihara, opened March 1964, 3,875 seats, wrestling venue) and Ashihara's tall reinforced-concrete Olympic control tower as skyline silhouettes (tower height unverified).

*Por que importa:* This is the actual room where women's Olympic team sport began. Its modesty is the point: a 2,359-seat concrete box, not a spectacle arena — the contrast between the small hall and the 66.8% national television audience is the emotional core of the exhibit.

#### Nichibo Kaizuka factory gymnasium — Daimatsu's training hall

A plain company gymnasium attached to the Dai Nippon Spinning mill at Kaizuka, Osaka. Build it as a long, low hall: worn wooden strip floor scarred and polished in the receiving zones, unpainted or cream-painted plaster/board walls, steel-sash industrial windows in a high band, bare pendant lamps, wall bars, a wall clock reading well past midnight. Props: several wicker or wire baskets and canvas sacks spilling white leather balls; a low bench with folded towels; a kettle and enamel cups; a blackboard with rotation diagrams; Daimatsu's whistle, stopwatch and a clipboard; sweat-darkened patches on the floor; a hand-lettered banner. Character staging: players mid-kaiten reshību — low body, one arm extended to dig, shoulder about to meet the boards, legs already whipping over to bring them back to their feet, the whole motion lifted from judo's ukemi break-fall. Costume here is the pre-uniform look: loose 'lantern' bloomers and plain cotton shirts. Daimatsu himself: dark trousers, white short-sleeved shirt, arms folded. The regime he ran was near-continuous — a single week off around Easter, supper at 19:00 routinely pushed back, players training after mill shifts that ended at 15:00 until roughly 02:00 in the last two years before Tokyo. A labour union objected to the intensity; Sports Illustrated ran a piece headlined 'Driven Beyond Dignity'; the players themselves defended him.

*Por que importa:* The gold medal was manufactured in this room, and the room is where the sport's most-copied defensive technique was invented. It also carries the exhibit's honest moral ambiguity — a triumph built on training that contemporaries already called abusive.

#### California beach doubles court set, c. 1950s–60s

Sand court of roughly 9 × 18 m with NO painted lines — boundaries were commonly marked with lengths of rope or garden hose pegged at the corners, or simply agreed. Posts: rough 4×4 timber uprights, weathered silver-grey, sunk directly into the sand and often braced with a guy line to a buried anchor or sandbag; net height set roughly to the indoor 2.43 m men's standard but visibly uneven. Net: the same 1 m × 9.5 m black square mesh with white canvas top and bottom bands, sagging noticeably in the middle, the canvas sun-bleached and salt-stained; NO antennae. Ball: the same white 18-panel leather indoor ball — this matters, because leather absorbed water and sand, darkened along the seams and grew heavier and harder through a day's play; model a beach ball variant with dirty grey-tan seam channels and a scuffed, slightly waxy surface. Surrounding set-dressing for the diorama: a wooden lifeguard tower on stilts with a ramp, hipped roof, and pale blue/white peeling paint; a red torpedo rescue buoy; striped canvas beach umbrellas in faded red/white and green/white; wool blankets; a transistor radio; a wooden-flanked 'woodie' station wagon and early longboards at the edge of frame; the Manhattan Beach Pier with its round concrete roundhouse on the horizon. Players: men bare-chested and barefoot in solid cotton-canvas swim trunks (navy, white or faded red, short 10–13 cm inseam, lace or belted front); women in two-piece suits or one-pieces with 1960s high-waist cuts. Deep tans, zinc on noses, no sunglasses on court, no branding anywhere.

*Por que importa:* While Europe and Japan were building the indoor Olympic game, California was inventing the other half of volleyball — two players, no substitutions, no coach, and a court with no lines. The 1960 Manhattan Beach Open and the first governing body (CBVA) both date to this room's decades.

#### Manual flip-card scoreboard and the side-out scoring rig

A freestanding or wall-hung scoreboard of the pre-electronic era: a timber or sheet-steel frame, roughly 1.5 m wide × 0.9 m tall, painted dark green or black, with two large windows of hinged number cards flipped by hand — white numerals on black card, 0 through 15, plus a smaller set-count strip showing games won. Team name plates are slotted-in painted boards or chalk. Alongside it, a second small rig on the officials' table: a hinged wooden set-score flipper and a scoresheet on a clipboard filled in with fountain pen. Include a hand-held brass whistle on a cord, a wooden line-judge flag (a small square of red cloth on a dowel), and a wooden referee stand with a single handrail. These details encode the rules of the moment: best-of-five sets to 15 points, SIDE-OUT scoring (a team could only score while serving, so scoreboards crawled and rallies could run for minutes), the block still counting as one of the team's three permitted hits — that changed only in 1976 — and no antennae bounding the crossing space.

*Por que importa:* Scoring is invisible in a 3D scene unless you build the object that displays it. A slow hand-flipped board is the truest indicator that this is side-out volleyball, a fundamentally different, more attritional game than the rally-scored sport visitors know.

### Marcos (30)

- **1947** [high] The Fédération Internationale de Volleyball (FIVB) is founded in Paris with 14 founding member federations. Its first act is to harmonise the divergent American and European codes: the court is fixed at 9 × 18 m and net heights are unified. (Secondary sources give the founding Congress as 18–20 April 1947 and name Paul Libaud of France as first president — see 'medium' entry below.)
- **1947** [medium] Founding Congress dates of 18–20 April 1947, Paul Libaud (France) elected first FIVB President, and the FIVB headquarters remaining in Paris for 37 years until 1984.
- **1948** [medium] The first European Championship is held in Rome and won by Czechoslovakia; rule clarifications this year address the definition of blocking and limits on the service.
- **1949** [high] The first FIVB Men's World Championship is played in Prague, Czechoslovakia, 10–18 September 1949. Ten teams compete (Uruguay and Yugoslavia withdrew). The Soviet Union wins the inaugural title undefeated (5–0 in the final round), Czechoslovakia takes silver and Bulgaria bronze. The tournament is opened by Czechoslovak President Klement Gottwald.
- **1949** [medium] Rule innovation credited to this period: setters are permitted to penetrate from the back line, enabling the three-player attack. In 1951 back-line players are permitted to spike from within their own zone.
- **1952** [high] The first FIVB Women's World Championship is played in Moscow, USSR, 17–29 August 1952. Eight teams play a single round robin. The Soviet Union wins every match (7–0, 21 sets to 0); Poland takes silver, Czechoslovakia bronze. The deciding USSR–Poland match is played at Dynamo Central Stadium. The men's championship is staged in the USSR the same year and is also won by the Soviet Union (Czechoslovakia silver, Bulgaria bronze, 11 teams).
- **1956** [high] The championships go global: 24 men's teams from four continents compete in Paris, France — Czechoslovakia takes men's gold ahead of Romania, with the USSR only third. The women's event, also in France with 17 teams, is won again by the Soviet Union ahead of Romania and Poland.
- **24 September 1957** [high] At its Session in Sofia, Bulgaria, the IOC recognises volleyball as an Olympic sport and the FIVB as the sole worldwide governing body of the discipline.
- **1960** [high] The World Championships are held outside Europe for the first time, in Brazil. The USSR wins the men's title (Czechoslovakia silver, Romania bronze, 14 teams) and the women's title (Japan silver, Czechoslovakia bronze, 10 teams). Japan's silver-medal loss to the Soviets is the defeat that pushes coach Hirofumi Daimatsu to build his speed-and-agility system.
- **1961** [high] On a European tour, the Nichibo Kaizuka-based Japanese women's team wins 24 consecutive matches against national teams. European media christen them the 'Oriental Witches' (東洋の魔女). An American television commentator popularises the phrase again during the 1964 Olympic final.
- **1961** [high] Volleyball is formally added to the Olympic programme as a medal sport for both men and women for the 1964 Tokyo Games.
- **1962** [high] The Women's World Championship is staged in the Soviet Union, 13–25 October 1962, across four cities (Kyiv, Leningrad, Moscow, Riga) with 14 nations. Japan beats the USSR in the deciding match to take its first world title, ending three straight Soviet championships. Soviet Union silver, Poland bronze.
- **1962** [high] The Men's World Championship, also in the USSR, is won by the Soviet Union (Czechoslovakia silver, Romania bronze, 21 teams) — the USSR's third men's world title.
- **11–23 October 1964** [high] Volleyball makes its Olympic debut at the Tokyo Games. 179 athletes from 11 nations compete across the two tournaments, played at the Komazawa Volleyball Courts (Komazawa Indoor Ball Sports Field, Setagaya, Tokyo) and the Yokohama Cultural Gymnasium. Women's volleyball is the first team sport ever contested by women at the Olympic Games.
- **1964** [high] Men's Olympic tournament (13–23 October, 10 teams, single round robin): GOLD Soviet Union (8–1, 25 sets won / 5 lost, set ratio 5.000); SILVER Czechoslovakia (8–1, 26/10, ratio 2.600 — separated only by set ratio); BRONZE Japan (7–2). The USSR's single defeat was to Japan, 1–3, on 19 October (14–16, 15–5, 15–8, 15–10 to Japan). The USSR had earlier survived Czechoslovakia 3–2 on 18 October (15–9, 15–8, 5–15, 10–15, 15–7).
- **1964** [high] Women's Olympic tournament (11–23 October, 6 teams: Japan, USSR, Poland, Romania, USA, South Korea): GOLD Japan (5–0, losing only one set in the whole event); SILVER Soviet Union (4–1); BRONZE Poland (3–2). Japan's roster: Masae Kasai (captain), Emiko Miyamoto, Kinuko Tanida, Yuriko Handa, Yoshiko Matsumura, Sata Isobe, Katsumi Matsumura, Yoko Shinozaki, Setsuko Sasaki, Yuko Fujimoto, Masako Kondo, Ayano Shibuki; coach Hirofumi Daimatsu. USSR roster included captain Lyudmila Buldakova, Antonina Ryzhova, Nelli Abramova and the 20-year-old debutante Inna Ryskal.
- **23 October 1964** [high] The Olympic women's final at the Komazawa Volleyball Courts: Japan beats the Soviet Union 3–0 (15–11, 15–8, 15–13), starting at approximately 19:36 and finishing around 21:01 after a 30-minute delay. The Japanese Olympic Committee records a television audience rating of 66.8% — at the time the highest for any sports broadcast in Japanese history. The final point came on a Soviet over-the-net violation.
- **1953** [high] Hirofumi Daimatsu (b. 12 February 1921, Kagawa Prefecture; a WWII second lieutenant who survived the Burma/Imphal campaign) becomes coach of the Nichibo Kaizuka women's team, the factory side of Dai Nippon Spinning Co. in Kaizuka, Osaka. His brutal regime earns him the name 'Demon Daimatsu'; his teams compile 175 consecutive victories. He retired from the national team in 1965 to help build a volleyball programme in China, was elected to Japan's House of Councillors in 1968, died 24 November 1978, and was enshrined in the International Volleyball Hall of Fame in 2000. (English Wikipedia dates the Nichibo Kaizuka team's establishment to 1954 — the 1953/1954 discrepancy is unresolved.)
- **1960–1964** [high] Daimatsu invents/codifies the 'rolling receive' (回転レシーブ, kaiten reshību) — a dive-and-roll dig adapted from judo's ukemi break-falls, in which the player plays the ball low, rolls over the shoulder or across the back without the seat touching the floor, and comes back onto her feet already moving. He also develops the 'floating' change-up serve. Both were engineered to let a short, light team beat the taller Soviets on speed. Players worked the mill until 15:00 and trained until roughly 02:00 in the final two years before the Games.
- **August 1966** [medium] The Oriental Witches' streak of consecutive official six-a-side victories — reported by Japanese sources as 258 matches — finally ends. (English-language sources more commonly cite the figure of 175 straight wins under Daimatsu; the two counts use different scopes.)
- **1930** [high] Beach volleyball's doubles format is born in Santa Monica, California: Paul 'Pablo' Johnson of the Santa Monica Athletic Club, short of players for a six-a-side game one summer day, splits the four people present into two two-man teams — the first recorded beach doubles game. (Beach play itself traces to 1915 at the Outrigger Canoe Club, Waikiki, and to Santa Monica courts built after the 1920 jetties widened the sand; eleven beach clubs existed by late 1922 and inter-club play began in 1924.)
- **1947** [medium] The first organised two-man beach volleyball tournament is held at State Beach, Santa Monica/Pacific Palisades, California (several sources say Will Rogers State Beach; the FIVB credits Bernie Holtzman as organiser). No prize money. In 1948 the first tournament with a prize is held in Los Angeles — the winners receive a case of Pepsi.
- **1950s** [medium] The first beach circuit is organised across five California beaches. Source lists differ: Santa Barbara, Will Rogers State Beach, Sorrento Beach, Laguna Beach and San Diego in one account; Corona del Mar, Santa Barbara, San Diego, Laguna and State Beach in another. Women begin forming two-woman teams and entering tournaments in this decade. Brazil holds its first newspaper-sponsored beach tournament.
- **1960** [high] The first Manhattan Beach Open is played on the south side of the Manhattan Beach Pier — an amateur event that becomes the longest-running and most prestigious beach tournament in the United States, later nicknamed the 'Wimbledon of beach volleyball'. Mike Bright and Mike O'Hara are among the dominant men's pairs of the 1960s.
- **1960s** [low] Beach volleyball becomes a mainstream California pastime with open tournaments running at thirteen major beaches. The California Beach Volleyball Association is founded as the sport's first governing body, to coordinate schedules and codify rules — dated 1965 by the Volleyball Hall of Fame timeline but 1962 by Wikipedia. President John F. Kennedy is reported to have attended an event at Sorrento Beach, Los Angeles.
- **1965** [high] The first FIVB Men's World Cup — the 'Tournament of the Continents' — is played in Poland, 13–19 September 1965, across five cities with 11 nations. The Soviet Union, coached by Yuri Kleshchev, wins ahead of Poland and Czechoslovakia, with Japan fourth as the only non-European entrant.
- **1966** [high] Czechoslovakia wins the Men's World Championship at home (Romania silver, USSR bronze, 22 teams). In 1967 Japan wins the Women's World Championship on home soil in a small four-team field (USA silver, South Korea bronze).
- **1968** [high] At the Mexico City Games (13–26 October 1968, 12 nations, 206 athletes; venues Gimnasio Olímpico Juan de la Barrera, Palacio de los Deportes and the Revolution Ice Rink) the Soviet Union sweeps both gold medals, with Japan runner-up in both events, Czechoslovakia and Poland taking a bronze each. Volleyball had initially been dropped from the 1968 programme before protests restored it.
- **1968** [medium] The use of antennae to limit the court's air space over the net is recommended to make refereeing decisions easier — so nets at Tokyo 1964 had NO antennae, a key visual difference from the modern game. The block continued to count as one of the team's three permitted hits until 1976, when it became a separate contact; the antenna spacing was also cut from 9.40 m to 9.00 m at the 1976 Montreal Games.
- **1957–1959** [low] Assorted rule housekeeping of the period: consideration of a second referee, time-outs limited to 1 minute and 30 seconds (1957); screening on the serve outlawed (1959). Sourced only from secondary volleyball-history compilations, not from an FIVB document.

### Correcoes do fact-check (16 | 22 marcos confirmados)

> Checked all 30 milestones and every iconicObjects claim against Wikipedia (English and Japanese), the FIVB/CEV tournament records, the IOC/Olympic venue lists and the Olympedia-derived tournament tables. WebSearch quota was exhausted at the outset, so all verification was done by direct WebFetch against named reference pages; a few targets (FIVB history page, volleyhall.org, olympic-museum.de, Mikasa product pages, Britannica) returned 404/403/timeouts and could not be used.

The competition history is in excellent shape. Every world-championship and Olympic result in the block checks out exactly: 1949 Prague (USSR/TCH/BUL, 10 teams, Gottwald), 1952 Moscow women (8 teams, 7-0, 21-0 sets, USSR/POL/TCH) and the 1952 men's (11 teams, USSR/TCH/BUL), 1956 France (men 24 teams TCH/ROU/URS; women 17 teams URS/ROU/POL), 1960 Brazil (men 14 URS/TCH/ROU; women 10 URS/JPN/TCH), 1962 USSR (women 13-25 Oct, 4 cities, 14 teams, Japan's first title; men 21 teams URS/TCH/ROU), 1965 World Cup in Poland (13-19 Sept, 5 cities, 11 nations, Kleshchev, URS/POL/TCH, Japan 4th and sole overseas entrant), 1966 and 1967, and 1968 Mexico City (13-26 Oct, 12 nations, 206 athletes, three venues, USSR double gold, Japan double silver, TCH and POL bronzes, plus the near-removal-and-protest story). The 1948 Rome European Championship won by Czechoslovakia is confirmed. FIVB 1947 is confirmed in full detail: Paris, congress 18-20 April, fourteen founding federations, Paul Libaud, headquarters in Paris until the 1984 move to Lausanne.

Tokyo 1964 is confirmed down to the set scores. Men's: 10 teams, USSR 8-1 (25-5, ratio 5.000), Czechoslovakia 8-1 (26-10, 2.600), Japan 7-2. I specifically re-verified the most surprising claim — that Japan beat the Soviet Union 3-1 on 19 October — because a first summarisation pass rendered it backwards; the Wikipedia match table reads "Japan 3-1 Soviet Union", and the USSR's 25-5 set line is only arithmetically possible if Japan won that match, so the block is right. Women's: six named teams, Japan 5-0 with exactly one set dropped (13-15 to Poland), USSR 4-1, Poland 3-2; final 3-0 (15-11, 15-8, 15-13) on 23 October ending on a Soviet over-the-net violation. Both the Japanese roster of twelve and the Soviet roster (Buldakova as captain, Ryzhova, Abramova, the 20-year-old Ryskal) are correct. Komazawa Indoor Ball Sports Field is confirmed precisely: December 1962 groundbreaking, March 1964 completion, reinforced concrete, 1,629 sq m at 35.4 x 46 m, and 2,355 seats plus 4 accessible = the 2,359 figure, against 3,908 in the Olympic listing. Daimatsu's biography checks out entirely (12 Feb 1921, Ayauta-gun Kagawa, Imphal, 1953, 175 straight wins, 1968 House of Councillors, died 24 Nov 1978, IVHF 2000), as does the Oriental Witches material (1961 European tour 24 straight wins, nickname from European media then re-popularised by an American broadcaster in 1964, 258-match official streak ended 6 August 1966 by Yashica, mill work then practice from 15:00 to 02:00). Beach volleyball's 1915 Outrigger origin, the 1920 jetties, eleven clubs by late 1922, inter-club play from 1924, Paul Johnson's 1930 doubles game, the 1948 Pepsi prize, the 1950s Brazilian newspaper tournament, the 1960 Manhattan Beach Open and the JFK anecdote are all present in the sources as described. Kamekura's authorship of the Tokyo 1964 emblem, listed as unverified, is in fact confirmed.

The errors cluster in the physical-object descriptions rather than the history. The most substantive are: the medal inscription placed on the wrong face and attached to a wrong sport count; the ball panel dimensions, which are geometrically impossible for a 65-67 cm ball; the Mikasa V200W cited as an example of the plain-white classic pattern when it is the current coloured dimpled ball, which contradicts the block's own anachronism warning; and the headline's "twelve textile-mill workers", when two of the twelve came from Kurashiki Spinning and from Yashica, an optics company. Two rule-history milestones (the 1949/1951 attack rules and the 1968/1976 antennae-and-block package) could not be corroborated anywhere, and the block's own openQuestions describes a *different* 1951 claim than the one its milestone actually makes. The 1961 Olympic-programme milestone is marked high confidence but no source I reached dates the decision to 1961. Finally, the net and court specification is presented as "1964-spec" while being lifted verbatim from the current FIVB rulebook — the same sourcing caveat the block correctly applies to the ball but omits here.

- **Alegacao:** Headline: 'twelve Japanese textile-mill workers beat the USSR in Tokyo'
  - **Problema:** The 1964 Japan roster was 12 players but only 10 came from Nichibo Kaizuka (Dai Nippon Spinning). Per the Oriental Witches article, one player came from Kurashiki Spinning and one from Yashica — a camera and optics manufacturer, not a textile mill. The 'all twelve were mill girls' framing is the single most-repeated popular simplification about this team.
  - **Correto:** Ten of the twelve Japanese players came from the Nichibo Kaizuka mill team; the other two came from Kurashiki Spinning and from Yashica (an optics/camera maker). Phrase it as 'a team built around ten textile-mill workers' rather than 'twelve textile-mill workers'.
- **Alegacao:** Headline: 'in front of two-thirds of their nation' (and, in the milestone, a JOC television audience rating of 66.8%)
  - **Problema:** 66.8% is a television RATING — the share of measured (Kanto-region) television households tuned in — not the share of the Japanese population watching. Converting it to 'two-thirds of their nation' is exactly the error the block's own openQuestions warns against when it rejects English Wikipedia's 'over 80% of the entire population'. Japanese Wikipedia gives a 67-85% range across measurement regions and services.
  - **Correto:** Say 'a 66.8% television rating — at the time the highest recorded for any Japanese broadcast'. Do not restate the rating as a fraction of the population.
- **Alegacao:** Tokyo 1964 medal: 'REVERSE: an Olympic champion being carried shoulder-high through a jubilant crowd, with the inscription XVIII OLYMPIAD TOKYO 1964 and the English names of the twenty sports set around the field.'
  - **Problema:** Two errors. (1) On the Cassioli design the host name and Games numeral sit on the OBVERSE — Wikipedia's Olympic medal article states the Nike/Colosseum obverse has 'a space left for the name of the Olympic host and the Games numeral'. The reverse is the crowd-carried athlete alone. (2) Tokyo 1964 had 19 sports (25 disciplines, 163 events), not twenty; and on Cassioli-design medals the individual sport was engraved on the edge/rim, not laid around the reverse field.
  - **Correto:** OBVERSE: Cassioli's Nike with palm and victor's crown, Colosseum behind, carrying the inscription XVIII OLYMPIAD TOKYO 1964. REVERSE: the champion carried shoulder-high by the crowd, with no games inscription. The sport was engraved on the rim. Tokyo 1964 comprised 19 sports.
- **Alegacao:** Tokyo 1964 medal: 'Disc 63 mm in diameter and 5 mm thick, weighing 103 g (gold and silver) or 102 g (bronze)... 314 gold, 314 silver and 319 bronze were produced.'
  - **Problema:** Wikipedia's Olympic medal article gives Tokyo 1964 as 60 mm diameter, 7.5 mm thick and 62 g, directly contradicting all three figures. The block flags the diameter conflict in openQuestions but presents the 103 g / 102 g weights and the mintage counts as settled fact, and 63 x 5 mm came from olympic-museum.de, which now 404s and could not be re-checked. Neither figure set is internally plausible either: a 60 mm x 7.5 mm solid silver disc would weigh roughly 220 g, so the 62 g value is also suspect.
  - **Correto:** Treat the medal's physical specification as unresolved. 60 mm is the more commonly cited diameter; the thickness (5 mm vs 7.5 mm) and the mass (62 g vs 103 g) are in open conflict across sources. Do not put any of these numbers on a plaque or drive a hero-asset model from them without the 1964 Official Report.
- **Alegacao:** Ball: 'Each panel is roughly 3.5 cm × 10.5 cm with softly rounded corners.'
  - **Problema:** Geometrically impossible for the ball the block itself specifies. With a 65-67 cm circumference, each of the six rosettes spans roughly a quarter of the circumference, i.e. about 16-17 cm on a side, so each of its three parallel panels is about 5.5 cm wide and 16.5 cm long. Panels of 3.5 x 10.5 cm would tile a ball of only about 42 cm circumference — a child's ball. Modelling to these numbers would produce a visibly wrong panel count density.

## 1970s–1980s: From the Eastern Bloc Dynasties to Sand, Spandex and the American Golden Age

**Volleyball's two decades of tectonic shift: the Soviet–Polish superpower era gave way to a rules rewrite that unshackled the blocker, a Brazilian serving revolution, an American team that reinvented passing and won everything, and a players' union on a Californian beach that turned the sport into television.**

### Paleta visual

TWO DISTINCT ROOMS.

1970s wing — 'The Iron Curtain Gymnasium'. Cool, austere, institutional. Palette: bone white, Polish red #D4213D, Soviet crimson #C1121C, ochre/burnt-orange floor #C56A2A, gymnasium hardwood honey-brown, olive and slate grey, chromium and brushed steel. Materials: heavyweight matte cotton, absorbent leather, painted steel tube, varnished wood, pressed metal, wired-glass windows, exposed girders. Lighting: high-mounted mercury-vapour and incandescent floodlights giving a slightly green-cyan cast, hard pools of light with deep unlit ceiling voids, visible dust in the beams, and the specular sheen of sweat and varnished floor. Typography: Univers and Helvetica set tight and symmetrical (Montreal 1976's own house typeface was Univers), Cyrillic grotesques for Soviet material, all-caps condensed sans for banners; single-colour printing. Architecture: 1960s–70s multipurpose arena — steep concrete bowl, moulded plastic tip-up seats in flat primary colours, a bulb-matrix scoreboard with amber segment digits, painted federation crests on cloth banners hung from the rafters, no LED, no video wall.

1980s wing — 'Sun, Sand and Spandex'. Hot, saturated, televisual. Palette: LA84 'Festive Federalism' magenta #E0218A, chrome yellow #FFC61E, aqua #2EC4B6, vermillion orange #F35B2B, with baby blue, purple and mint-green accents; plus beach neutrals — bleached sand #E4D5B7, Pacific blue, and the neon coral/teal/hot-pink of Redsand-era beachwear. Materials: lycra and nylon, vinyl banner stock, painted cardboard sonotubes, scaffold tube, moulded plastic, anodised aluminium, sun-faded webbing. Lighting: two moods — arena metal-halide, cool-white and even, flattened for colour television with visible lens flares and the pop of press flashguns; and hard midday coastal sun, near-vertical, bleaching the sand to white, throwing short black shadows, backlit sea haze and a warm rim on every silhouette. Typography: heavy geometric and rounded sans (Futura Bold, Eurostile, Serpentine), airbrushed gradients, chrome-and-neon lettering, drop shadows, angled 'speed' logotypes. Architecture: temporary and modular — scaffold towers, low bleachers, guy-wired net posts in sand, banner-wrapped rails, folding tables and umbrellas, Taraflex mats rolled over concrete indoors. The 1970s room should feel enclosed, heavy and monumental; the 1980s room should feel open, light, plastic and disposable — a sport that had escaped the gymnasium.

### Objetos de acervo (9)

#### FIVB 18-panel white leather indoor match ball (c. 1970–1989)

Sphere, circumference 65–67 cm — i.e. 20.7–21.3 cm (207–213 mm) diameter — mass 260–280 g, inflated to 0.30–0.325 kg/cm² (4.26–4.61 psi), so it holds a firm, near-perfect sphere with only slight faceting at the seams. Skin is 18 nearly-rectangular panels of soft full-grain cowhide, arranged as SIX identical groups of THREE parallel panels, each group rotated 90° to its neighbours (the classic 'basket-weave' or pinwheel layout). Each panel is roughly 6.5 cm × 11 cm with rounded corners; panels are hand-stitched and turned, giving recessed seam channels about 2–3 mm deep and 3 mm wide, slightly darker than the panel faces from grime and hand oil. Colour: uniform off-white / bone leather (NOT the tri-colour yellow-blue-white ball, which was not adopted as an FIVB world-championship ball until 1998). Branding is minimal by modern standards — a small maker's oval (MIKASA, or TACHIKARA SV-5W for US domestic play) hot-stamped in navy or gold on one panel, plus a one-line 'OFFICIAL' or federation legend on the opposite pole. A short valve nipple sits flush in one seam junction. For wear: scuff the equator, grey the seams, and let the leather take a very slightly ovoid, hand-worn softness — 1980s leather balls visibly aged over a tournament.

*Por que importa:* This is the single object that dates the room. A white 18-panel leather ball says 'pre-1998' instantly, and its softness and water-absorbency is exactly why beach and indoor eventually diverged. Mikasa signed with the FIVB in 1970, so the whole era is bracketed by this ball.

#### Post-1976 net, antenna and post assembly (the block-touch-era net)

Net panel: 1.00 m tall, 9.50–10.00 m long, of 10 cm square BLACK knotted mesh. Top band: 7 cm wide doubled white canvas sewn along the full length, with a flexible steel cable threaded inside; bottom band 5 cm wide white canvas with a rope inside, tensioned to the posts. Two white SIDE BANDS, 5 cm wide × 1.00 m tall, are sewn vertically at the ends, placed directly above each sideline. Antennae: flexible fibreglass rods 1.80 m long × 10 mm diameter, fastened to the OUTER edge of each side band on opposite faces of the net, with exactly 80 cm projecting above the top band; painted in alternating 10 cm bands of red and white (8 bands visible above the net). Posts: 2.55 m tall, rounded, smooth, no guy wires, set 1.00 m outside each sideline (FIVB competition), typically padded from floor to ~2 m with a cylindrical vinyl sleeve. Net height 2.43 m for men, 2.24 m for women, measured at the centre; it may sag no more than 2 cm above official height at the sidelines. CRITICAL MUSEUM DETAIL: for a 'before 1976' exhibit, place the antennae 9.40 m apart — outside the sidelines, floating over the free zone; for 'after 1976', 9.00 m apart, standing exactly over the sidelines. Two nets side by side with a measuring tape between them is the whole rule change made physical.

*Por que importa:* The 1974 Mexico City Congress decision that took effect in 1976 did two things at once — narrowed the crossing space to 9.00 m and stopped the block touch counting as a team hit. It is the most consequential rules moment of the 1970s and it is visible in one prop.

#### AVP beach volleyball court, c. 1985–1988

IMPORTANT AND OFTEN MODELLED WRONG: in the 1980s the two-man beach court was still the INDOOR footprint — 9 m × 18 m of sand, not today's 8 m × 16 m. (The reduction to 8 × 16 came in 2001–02, together with rally scoring.) Boundaries are not painted: they are flat woven polypropylene webbing tapes roughly 5 cm wide in a contrasting colour (safety orange, yellow or blue), pegged at the four corners with sand anchors and tensioned so they lie half-buried; the tapes sag and drift and are constantly re-set by players' feet. NO centre line, no attack line, no free-zone markings. Sand: pale, coarse, well-sifted, at least ~40 cm deep, sculpted into foot craters, dig trenches and thrown sprays around the net. Posts are round steel, padded, driven or guy-wired into the sand — 1980s rigs often had visible steel cable guys running out to buried deadmen, unlike the free-standing indoor posts. Ball: the same white leather 18-panel indoor-spec ball (65–67 cm, 0.30–0.325 kg/cm²) — the beach-specific softer, larger, lower-pressure ball only arrived in the 1990s. Surroundings: a single wooden or aluminium referee stand at one post; low scaffold bleachers on two sides; vinyl sponsor banners zip-tied to scaffold rails and to the courtside fence, reading JOSE CUERVO, MILLER LITE, BOLLE; a folding scorer's table with a manual flip scoreboard and a beach umbrella; a PA horn on a mast.

*Por que importa:* This is the physical form of professionalisation. The AVP was founded on 21 July 1983 as a players' union, struck at Redondo Beach in 1984, ran its own tour from 1984, hit US$275,000 across eight states in 1985, and reached national television on ABC's Wide World of Sports in 1986. Everything about the setup — webbing lines, guy-wired posts, a leather indoor ball soaking up salt air — is scrappy in a way the modern tour is not.

#### Manhattan Beach Pier 'Volleyball Walk of Fame' plaque

#### Taraflex portable court panel, referee stand and manual flip scoreboard (indoor arena kit)

Court surface: rolled synthetic vinyl sports flooring laid over an arena's concrete or hardwood, typically in two tones — a saturated playing area (deep ocean blue, brick red or a 1970s burnt orange/ochre) with a contrasting free zone — with 5 cm white line tape defining the 18 × 9 m court, the two 3 m attack lines, and short broken extension marks. The surface has a fine embossed grain and a low satin sheen that squeaks and shows shoe-rubber scuffs and skid arcs. *Por que importa:* Taraflex was laid for volleyball at Montreal 1976 and has been at every Summer Games since, so the coloured synthetic mat is the visual signature of the modern era's arrival. #### Los Angeles 1984 'Festive Federalism' venue decoration kit

A modular 'kit of parts' of temporary structures in a palette of magenta, chrome yellow, aqua and vermillion orange, with accents of baby blue, purple and mint green. Core elements: SONOTUBES — cardboard-formwork cylinders roughly 60–90 cm in diameter and 3–5 m tall, standing in clusters, painted in flat bands, contrasting hoops, splattered Pollock-style flecks, stars or sport pictograms; orange scaffold towers hung with teal and silver spheres; yellow gazebos raised on purple and mint-green columns; temporary entry pediments and stripped-classical facades in painted plywood; nylon street banners in tall vertical proportions; matching tents for concessions and first aid. *Por que importa:* It is the reason the 1984 room and the 1976 room must not look alike. Montreal is clean modernist red-on-white European rigour; Los Angeles is sun-bleached postmodern pastel scaffolding. Volleyball's American breakout happened inside that second aesthetic, and the AVP beach look grew straight out of it.

#### 1980s knee pads, court shoes and the Redsand-era beachwear rack

Knee pads: two generations side by side — the 1970s pad, a thin low white cotton-covered foam doughnut about 8 cm tall that slides down the shin; and the 1980s pad, a taller 14–16 cm ribbed nylon sleeve over layered closed-cell foam, in white, black or team colour, with a slight contoured cup over the patella. Court shoes: 1970s — flat white canvas or thin leather low-tops on a plain gum sole, almost no midsole, thin three-stripe or plain toe cap; 1980s — white leather low-tops with a visible EVA midsole wedge, a moulded heel counter, and a saturated single-colour side logo panel (ASICS, Mizuno, Nike), noticeably bulkier. *Por que importa:* Protective gear and footwear are the invisible enablers of the era's technique shift — deeper digging, harder landings, the back-row attack. And the apparel is the moment volleyball became a lifestyle product with its own brands, not just a sport.

Also note a framing trap: the 1984 US$300,000 figure belongs to Event Concepts' Pro Beach Tour, while the 1985 US$275,000 belongs to the new AVP-run tour, so the two must not be presented as continuous growth.
  - **Correto:** 1985: the AVP's own tour reaches eight US states with US$275,000 in prize money (confirmed) — note this is a new circuit, not a continuation of the US$300,000 1984 Pro Beach Tour. Treat the specific state list, the Bolle 1985 sponsorship and the Redsand 1985 founding year as unverified placeholders.

## 1990s

**The decade volleyball stopped being a gym sport and became a television product: the FIVB invented annual money competitions (World League 1990, World Grand Prix 1993), put beach volleyball in the Olympics at Atlanta 1996, and then rewrote the game itself in 1998 with the libero, the yellow-blue-white ball and rally-point scoring to 25.**

### Paleta visual

COLOUR — the decade is loud and hard-edged. Core hero colours: the tri-colour ball's golden yellow #F6C324, royal/ultramarine blue #143C9C and off-white #F4F3EE. National palettes to seed the crowd and kit racks: Italy azzurro #0F52A0 with white and red; Cuba red #CF142B with #002A8F and white; Brazil canary #FFDF00 with green #009739 and blue #002776; Netherlands orange #F36C21; Russia white/blue/red. Around them, early-90s sportswear hypercolour: teal #009B9B, magenta #D6217F, violet #6B3FA0, neon lime #C4E538, applied as hard geometric zigzags, confetti speckle and diagonal colour-blocking on white grounds. MATERIALS — shiny polyester tricot and open mesh, matte PU synthetic leather, foam post padding in matte printed vinyl, lacquered light-maple or PVC roll-out flooring in slate blue / teal with contrasting red or orange court lines, black rubber-topped folding tables, powder-coated steel scaffold, aluminium bench seating, glossy plastic bucket seats in primary colours. LIGHTING — banks of metal-halide high-bays with a slightly greenish-cyan cast, ~4200-5000 K, uneven pool coverage so the corners of the free zone fall off into gloom; hard specular hotspots on sweaty skin and on the ball's polished panels, deep unlit upper stands with light spill on faces only, faint haze in the beam. ### Objetos de acervo (8)

#### Mikasa tri-colour indoor match ball (yellow / blue / white), 1998 World Championship, Japan

A sphere of circumference 65-67 cm — model at radius 10.4 cm (diameter ~20.8 cm) — mass 260-280 g, inflated to 0.30-0.325 kgf/cm2 (4.26-4.61 psi), so the surface should read as taut but with a very slight give at the panel centres. Skin: 18 near-rectangular panels arranged as SIX square 'faces' of THREE parallel strips each; each face is rotated 90 degrees relative to its neighbours, so the seam network reads like a woven cube projected onto a sphere (the classic volleyball topology — do not use a football/soccer hex-pent pattern). Each strip is roughly 6.5-7 cm long by 3.3-3.5 cm wide at its centre, with rounded 6 mm corner radii. Seams are recessed valleys ~2.5 mm wide and ~1.5 mm deep with a visible double row of tight machine stitching (or a heat-bonded groove on the composite version); the panel edges bevel down into the valley over ~3 mm. Colour: two opposing faces in warm golden yellow (~#F6C324), two in royal/ultramarine blue (~#143C9C), two in off-white (~#F4F3EE), arranged so that from any camera angle you see yellow, blue and white simultaneously — that is the whole point of the design. Material: matte-to-satin synthetic (micro-fibre composite) leather with a fine pebble / orange-peel grain at ~0.4 mm scale; roughness ~0.55, no clearcoat, but slightly glossier polished streaks along the seams and on the yellow panels from ball-shine wear. Markings: black 'MIKASA' wordmark ~4.5 cm wide on one white panel; a small gold-and-blue FIVB roundel with 'OFFICIAL GAME BALL' text near the opposite pole; a flush black valve dot 8 mm across, slightly darker than the surrounding panel.

*Por que importa:* The single most recognisable visual break in volleyball's history. Before 1998 the world final was played with a white ball; the FIVB introduced the yellow-blue-white ball at the 1998 World Championships in Japan and colour has been the FIVB standard ever since. It was a broadcast decision as much as a sporting one: the tri-colour ball spins visibly on a 4:3 SD television picture, so viewers could finally read topspin and float serves.

#### All-white 18-panel leather match ball (the pre-1998 ball)

Identical 18-panel / six-face geometry and identical official dimensions (65-67 cm circumference, 260-280 g), but every panel in the same off-white genuine leather, ~#F2EFE6 fresh and yellowing to ~#E6E0CF at the seams and wear points. Visibly softer and more matte than the composite ball, with a slightly irregular sphere (2-3 mm out of round is correct), thicker raised seams, and stitching thread in a warm cream or pale tan that has darkened with handling. A round reinforcement patch about 3 cm across sits over the valve. Scuffs are grey-black rubber transfer from the floor, concentrated in a band. Model it a touch larger-looking and heavier-reading than the composite ball.

*Por que importa:* The 'before' object. Displayed in the same case as the tri-colour ball, it makes the 1998 change legible in one glance — this is the exhibit that explains why the room is lit the way it is and why every photo taken after November 1998 looks different.

#### Italy men's national team match kit, 'Generazione di Fenomeni' (1990-1998)

JERSEY: boxy, straight-cut, short-sleeved polyester tricot/mesh shirt — 1990s cut, not modern compression. Body hangs to mid-hip with a generous drop over the chest; sleeves end just above the elbow with a 2 cm hemmed cuff. Base azzurro (~#0F52A0) with white raglan-style sleeve panels and sublimated hard-edged geometric blocking across the chest — thin white and red chevrons/zigzags, the loud early-90s graphic idiom. Ribbed crew or shallow V collar 2 cm wide in white. Front number ~18-20 cm tall, back number ~28 cm tall, heavy block sans-serif in white with a 6 mm red outline. Left chest: federation shield ~7 cm tall; right chest: manufacturer logo ~5 cm (Asics/Mizuno-era wordmark). SHORTS: matching blue, elasticated waistband ~3.5 cm with a flat white drawcord, ~20 cm inseam so the hem sits at mid-thigh, 6 cm side split with contrast piping. KNEE PADS: white or black tubular ribbed-knit sleeves 18-20 cm tall with a 12-15 mm EVA foam disc over the patella, worn bunched low around the mid-shin between rallies. SHOES: low-cut white indoor court shoe, white or gum herringbone rubber outsole, chunky EVA midsole ~3 cm at the heel, royal/red synthetic overlays, 5-6 flat eyelets, thick padded tongue.

*Por que importa:* Italy won the men's World Championship in 1990, 1994 and 1998 and eight of the decade's ten World Leagues under Julio Velasco. This shirt is the uniform of the most dominant men's programme in the sport's history, and its baggy cut plus sublimated zigzag graphics is the fastest way to date any volleyball image to the 1990s.

#### Cuba women's national team kit — 'Las Morenas del Caribe'

TOP: fitted sleeveless or cap-sleeve shirt in Cuban red (~#CF142B) with white and royal blue (~#002A8F) flag-derived accents — typically a white shoulder yoke or diagonal side flash and thin blue piping. 'CUBA' arched across the chest in white block capitals ~5 cm tall, number below it ~14 cm, back number ~26 cm. BOTTOM: high-cut briefs or short spandex, 5-8 cm inseam, matching red with a single white side stripe 2 cm wide — the women's silhouette of the decade, distinctly briefer than the men's shorts. Knee pads in white, ribbed, 18 cm tall. Shoes low-cut white with red overlays. Two named display shirts: No. 3 for Mireya Luis (1.75 m outside hitter, spike reach 3.39 m — the highest recorded) and Regla Torres' shirt from 1992, when she was 17.

*Por que importa:* Cuba won Olympic gold in 1992, 1996 and 2000 — the only team ever to take three consecutive Olympic women's indoor titles — inside an unbroken run of eight straight FIVB world titles from the 1991 World Cup to the 2000 Olympics. Regla Torres was named the FIVB's best female player of the century and remains the youngest volleyball Olympic gold medallist ever.

#### The first libero jersey (1998)

Cut, fabric, number typography, crest placement and manufacturer marks identical to the team shirt beside it — the point is that only the COLOUR differs, and it must clash. Model a pair: the team shirt in navy or azzurro, the libero shirt in fluorescent yellow, orange or scarlet, hung on adjacent torso mannequins so the contrast is unmissable. Same 1990s boxy cut, same ~28 cm back number in block sans-serif, same ~7 cm chest crest. Add a small period detail: the libero's number is on the official roster card pinned beside it, and the shirt shows no captain's stripe (the original libero could not be team captain).

*Por que importa:* The libero was trialled from 1996 and written into the Official Rules for 1998 — a defensive specialist who wears a different-coloured uniform, substitutes freely into the back court without counting against substitutions, and may not serve, spike the ball over the net, block, or rotate into the front line. It is the only position in volleyball you can identify from a still photograph, and it exists because the 1990s game had become so attack-dominated that rallies were dying on the first ball.

#### Net, antennae, posts and referee stand (indoor, 1990s configuration)

NET: black knotted mesh, 10 cm square openings, 1 m deep, 9.5-10 m long. A 7 cm wide white canvas band runs along the top with a steel tension cable inside it, and a 5 cm white band along the bottom with a cord. Two 5 cm wide white vertical side bands are sewn at the ends, aligned exactly with the sidelines. ANTENNAE: flexible fibreglass rods 1.80 m long, 10 mm diameter, painted in alternating 10 cm red and white bands, fastened on the outer edge of each side band and protruding 80 cm above the net tape. NET HEIGHT: 2.43 m men, 2.24 m women (measured at the centre). POSTS: round steel, 2.55 m tall, set 0.5-1 m outside each sideline, sleeved in cylindrical foam padding ~15 cm thick wrapped in matte printed vinyl carrying sponsor logos. REFEREE STAND: adjustable steel platform with a small padded rail and a foam-clad front face, set so the first referee's eyes sit ~50 cm above the net tape; a second referee stands opposite at floor level. COURT: 18 m x 9 m, 5 cm wide painted lines, attack lines 3 m either side of the centre line, on lacquered light maple or a PVC roll-out sports surface. Free zone of at least 3 m all round, and considerably more at FIVB events.

*Por que importa:* The armature of the whole room: everything else in the exhibit — the ball, the kits, the libero, the scoreboards — is defined relative to these dimensions. The red-and-white antennae are also the single most photographable piece of volleyball hardware and read instantly as 'volleyball' in a 3D scene.

#### Atlanta 1996 beach volleyball court and its temporary stadium

A temporary open-air stadium of scaffold grandstands seating 10,000, erected at 'Atlanta Beach' in Clayton County International Park, Jonesboro, Georgia — sold out for all six days, 107,000+ spectators total. Steel scaffold understructure, aluminium bench and bucket seating in Atlanta-1996 palette (gold, green, purple, teal), white tensioned shade canopies over the VIP and press decks, banks of flat printed vinyl sponsor boards around the court, no LED anywhere. COURT: a graded sand pit of imported pale quartz sand, reported at around 4,000 tons trucked in from Fort Lauderdale, Florida, and at least 40 cm deep. Playing area 9 m x 18 m (the pre-2001 size — the reduction to 8 m x 16 m only came in 2001), marked ONLY by 5-8 cm wide coloured webbing tape (orange or blue) tensioned to four buried corner anchors: no attack line, no centre line, no painted marks. Net height 2.43 m men / 2.24 m women on padded posts. KIT: men in loose sleeveless tanks or short-sleeve tees with mid-thigh board shorts, sunglasses, visors or backward caps, barefoot, zinc on the nose; women in two-piece suits with visors and sunglasses. BALL: 18-panel Mikasa beach ball, softer and lower-pressure than the indoor ball, all-white or white with a single coloured swirl. Loose sand should mound along the tape lines and pile at the base of the net posts.

*Por que importa:* Beach volleyball's Olympic debut, 23-28 July 1996 — the moment the sport got a second, televisually irresistible half. Karch Kiraly and Kent Steffes beat Mike Dodd and Mike Whitmarsh 12-5, 12-8 in an all-American men's final, making Kiraly the first person with Olympic golds both indoors (1984, 1988) and on sand; Jackie Silva and Sandra Pires beat Mônica Rodrigues and Adriana Samuel in an all-Brazilian women's final.

#### The scoring apparatus — courtside scoreboard, scorer's table and the 1998 rulebook

SCOREBOARD: an electromechanical / early-LED courtside unit roughly 2.4 m wide x 1.2 m tall x 25 cm deep, dark grey or black powder-coated steel face with a 4 cm bezel. Two running-score windows with amber or red seven-segment digits ~30 cm tall (two digits each), team-name strips in white vinyl lettering on black slide-in cards, a 'SETS' row of five smaller windows with 8 cm digits, and small round indicator lamps for timeouts and service. A single chrome horn button on the operator's side, ribbon cable running to a desktop console. Optional detail: the digits' individual segments should render as slightly uneven, with a faint amber bloom and visible unlit segment ghosts. SCORER'S TABLE: a black rubber-topped folding table with a paper FIVB scoresheet on a clipboard, a manual flip-card scoreboard as backup, two whistles, a red/yellow card wallet, and a wire basket holding three match balls. THE MUSEUM GAG: place TWO identical scoreboards side by side, one frozen at 15-13 (side-out era) and one at 25-23 (rally era, from 1999), with the printed 1998 rules amendment mounted between them.

*Por que importa:* The rally-point system, ratified at the FIVB World Congress in October 1998, is the most consequential rule change in the sport's modern history: every rally now scores, the first four sets go to 25 (win by two) and the fifth to 15. Matches became time-predictable, which is what made volleyball schedulable for broadcasters — the same commercial logic that produced the World League in 1990 and the coloured ball in 1998. Two scoreboards tell that whole story without a word of text.

### Marcos (27)

- **1990** [high] The FIVB created the Volleyball World League, its first annual men's competition, explicitly as a marketing vehicle to make volleyball an annual TV property. The inaugural edition ran 27 April – 15 July 1990 with 8 nations (Pool A: Brazil, France, Italy, USA; Pool B: China, Japan, Netherlands, Soviet Union), with the Final Round at the Osaka Prefectural Gymnasium, Osaka, Japan. Italy beat the Netherlands 3-0 (15-7, 16-14, 16-14); Brazil third, USSR fourth. MVP: Andrea Zorzi (ITA); Best Spiker Ron Zwerver (NED); Best Blocker Andrea Gardini (ITA); Best Setter Paolo Tofoli (ITA).
- **1990** [medium] Reported total prize purse of the first World League was US$1 million, an unprecedented sum for volleyball at the time; the competition later ran 28 editions (1990-2017) before being replaced by the Nations League.
- **1990** [high] 18-28 October 1990, Brazil (Rio de Janeiro, Brasília, Curitiba): Italy won its FIRST men's World Championship, 16 teams, beating Cuba 3-1 in the final (12-15, 15-11, 15-6, 16-14). USSR bronze. This is the start of Julio Velasco's 'Generazione di Fenomeni'.
- **1989-1996** [high] Julio Velasco coached the Italy men's national team from 1989 to 1996, winning World Championships 1990 and 1994; World League 1990, 1991, 1992, 1994 and 1995; European Championships 1989, 1993 and 1995; the 1991 Mediterranean Games; the 1993 Grand Champions Cup; and Olympic silver in 1996. He was inducted into the International Volleyball Hall of Fame in 2003. Core players: Andrea Zorzi, Lorenzo Bernardi, Andrea Gardini, Andrea Giani, Paolo Tofoli.
- **1992** [high] Barcelona Olympics (26 July - 9 August 1992; Palau Sant Jordi, Palau dels Esports and Pavelló de la Vall d'Hebron): Brazil won the men's indoor gold (its first volleyball gold), Netherlands silver, USA bronze. Cuba won the women's gold — the first of three in a row — with the Unified Team silver and the USA bronze. Italy's men, then World Champions, finished 5th.
- **1992** [high] Regla Torres (b. 12 February 1975) was 17 at Barcelona 1992 and became the youngest Olympic gold medallist in volleyball history. She was later named Best Volleyball Player of the Century by the FIVB and inducted into the International Volleyball Hall of Fame in 2001.
- **1993** [high] The FIVB created the World Grand Prix, the women's counterpart to the World League, modelled on it three years later. The first edition ran 28 May – 30 June 1993 with 8 teams (Cuba, Brazil, China, South Korea, Russia, Japan, USA, Germany) and a Final Round in Hong Kong. Cuba won, China second, Russia third; MVP Mireya Luis (CUB), Best Setter Fernanda Venturini (BRA).
- **1993** [medium] At an IOC Executive Board meeting in September 1993 in Monaco, men's and women's beach volleyball were approved on a trial basis for the 1996 Atlanta Games. (Sourced from a search summary of the IOC/Olympic reference factsheet rather than a page I fetched in full.)
- **1994** [high] 29 September – 8 October 1994, Greece (Piraeus and Thessaloniki), 16 teams: Italy retained the men's World Championship, beating the Netherlands 3-1 (15-10, 11-15, 15-11, 15-1); USA bronze. MVP: Lorenzo Bernardi (ITA).
- **1994-1995** [medium] Legalisation of whole-body contact: the FIVB World Congress in Athens approved a rules package taking effect on 1 January 1995 that allowed the ball to be played with ANY part of the body, including the feet; the same package widened the service zone to the full 9 m behind the end line and removed the double-contact fault on the first ball off an opponent's attack. NOTE: sources conflict — Wikipedia's volleyball article dates the FIVB whole-body amendment to 1993 (and NCAA's to 1999), while the Athens-Congress account dates approval to 1994 with effect from 1995. Do not carve a single year on the plaque without a rulebook check.
- **1994** [high] Cuba won the women's World Championship in Brazil, part of an unbroken run of eight consecutive FIVB world titles across World Cup, Olympics and World Championship: 1991 World Cup, 1992 Olympics, 1994 World Championship, 1995 World Cup, 1996 Olympics, 1998 World Championship, 1999 World Cup, 2000 Olympics.
- **1995** [high] Italy's men won the World Cup in Japan with a perfect 11-0 record, completing the Velasco sweep of every major men's title of the era except the Olympics.
- **1994** [high] On 24 September 1994, at the IOC meeting in Monte Carlo, Monaco, beach volleyball received formal recognition as an Olympic discipline (per the FIVB's own beach volleyball history timeline).
- **1996** [high] BEACH VOLLEYBALL'S OLYMPIC DEBUT, Atlanta 1996. Venue: 'Atlanta Beach' at Clayton County International Park, Jonesboro, Georgia — a temporary 10,000-seat stadium that sold out all six days for more than 107,000 spectators. 24 men's teams (48 competitors from 19 nations) played 23-28 July; 18 women's pairs from 13 nations played 23-27 July. Format was double elimination; early matches were a single side-out game to 15, and the gold and bronze medal matches were upgraded to two sets to 12 with no win-by-two requirement.
- **1996** [high] Atlanta 1996 beach volleyball MEN (final 28 July): GOLD Karch Kiraly / Kent Steffes (USA), who beat fellow Americans Mike Dodd / Mike Whitmarsh 12-5, 12-8 in an all-USA final of just over an hour; BRONZE John Child / Mark Heese (CAN) over João Brenha / Miguel Maia (POR) 12-5, 12-8. Kiraly had already won indoor Olympic gold in 1984 and 1988, making him the first player with Olympic golds indoors and on sand. In the quarter-final Kiraly/Steffes survived Sinjin Smith / Carl Henkel 17-15 in 55 minutes after facing match point twice.
- **1996** [high] Atlanta 1996 beach volleyball WOMEN (final 27 July): GOLD Jackie Silva / Sandra Pires (BRA), SILVER Mônica Rodrigues / Adriana Samuel (BRA) in an all-Brazilian final; BRONZE Natalie Cook / Kerri Pottharst (AUS), who beat Barbra Fontana / Linda Hanley (USA) 12-11, 12-7 in a 111-minute match.
- **1996** [high] Atlanta 1996 INDOOR: the men's final on 4 August at the Omni Coliseum (capacity 16,378) saw the Netherlands beat Italy 3-2 (15-12, 9-15, 16-14, 9-15, 17-15) — one of the greatest Olympic finals ever played; FR Yugoslavia took bronze over Russia 3-1. The women's final on 3 August: Cuba beat China 3-1 for its second straight gold, Brazil beating Russia 3-2 for bronze. Tournament MVP: Cintha Boersma (NED); Best Blocker Magaly Carvajal (CUB); Best Spiker Cui Yongmei (CHN). Julio Velasco left the Italy job after these Games.
- **1996-1998** [medium] THE LIBERO. The FIVB's own 'The Game' history page states the specialised defensive player was introduced in 1996 (following the Atlanta Olympics, in response to offence-dominated play, and trialled in FIVB events including the women's World Grand Prix); the FIVB's 'Basic Rules' page and Wikipedia both date the formal introduction into international play to 1998, which is when the position entered the Official Rules and appeared at the World Championships in Japan. The safest museum wording: TRIALLED FROM 1996, ADOPTED INTO THE OFFICIAL RULES IN 1998. The original restrictions: must wear a differently coloured uniform from the rest of the team, may be substituted in the back court for any player without counting against substitutions, and may not serve, spike the ball over the net, block, or rotate into front-line positions. NCAA adoption came in 2002.
- **1997** [high] The first FIVB Beach Volleyball World Championships (men's and women's) were held on the UCLA campus in Los Angeles, USA, presented by Ericsson and Nike — the professional beach circuit having grown out of the first FIVB-sanctioned international event on Ipanema Beach, Rio de Janeiro, in 1987 and the three-event Beach Volleyball World Series of 1989/1990.
- **1998** [high] THE COLOURED BALL. In the FIVB's own words: after testing many colours, the FIVB introduced a ball with YELLOW, BLUE AND WHITE panels at the World Championships in Japan in 1998, replacing the all-white ball. Mikasa's own corporate history confirms 1998 as the year its coloured volleyballs were adopted as the official match ball of the World Championship, after which coloured balls became the FIVB standard and the visual signature of the brand. The rulebook itself permits this: the ball's 'colour may be a uniform light colour, or a combination of colours.'
- **1998** [high] Women's World Championship, 3-12 November 1998, Japan (Tokyo, Tokuyama, Matsumoto, Kagoshima, Nagoya, Fukuoka, Osaka), 16 teams: Cuba beat China 3-0 in the final — 15-4, 16-14, 15-12 — with Russia third. MVP: Regla Torres (CUB). The 15-point set scores are useful evidence for the museum: as late as November 1998 the world final was still being played under SIDE-OUT scoring to 15.
- **1998** [high] Men's World Championship, 13-29 November 1998, Japan (11 host cities: Fukuoka, Kobe, Sendai, Sapporo, Kawasaki, Uozu, Hiroshima, Osaka, Chiba, Hamamatsu, Tokyo), 24 teams: Italy won its THIRD consecutive world title (1990, 1994, 1998); Yugoslavia silver, Russia bronze. MVP: Rafael Pascual (ESP).
- **1998** [high] RALLY-POINT SCORING. At its World Congress in October 1998 the FIVB ratified the 'rally point system' — every rally now scores a point, regardless of who served, replacing side-out scoring in which only the serving team could score. The new format: the first four sets are played to 25 points, and the winning team must be ahead by at least two; the deciding fifth set is played to 15. It was adopted for a two-year test period.
- **1999-2000** [high] Implementation: the FIVB changed the rules in 1999 and the rally-point system became COMPULSORY in 2000; USA Volleyball's Rules of the Game Commission adopted matching changes in February 1999, effective for the 1999-2000 season from 1 November 1999. So on a plaque: RATIFIED OCTOBER 1998, PHASED IN 1999, MANDATORY 2000. The related change allowing a serve to touch the net and still be in play ('let serve') came with the 2000 rules package, not the 1998 one.
- **1999** [high] World League honours across the decade: Italy 1990, 1991, 1992; Brazil 1993; Italy 1994, 1995; Netherlands 1996; Italy 1997; Cuba 1998; Italy 1999 — eight of the decade's ten titles to Italy. World Grand Prix honours: Cuba 1993, Brazil 1994, USA 1995, Brazil 1996, Russia 1997, Brazil 1998, Russia 1999.
- **1999-2001** [medium] End-of-century canonisation: the FIVB named Regla Torres the best female volleyball player of the 20th century and Karch Kiraly the finest male player of the century; both were inducted into the International Volleyball Hall of Fame in Holyoke, Massachusetts in 2001. Mireya Luis (b. 25 August 1967, 1.75 m, outside hitter, No. 3, with a 3.39 m spike reach — the highest recorded of any player) followed in 2004. Cuba's coaches of the era were Eugenio George Lafita, then Antonio Perdomo, then Luis Felipe Calderón.
- **2001** [medium] Boundary marker for the room: the beach court was still 9 m x 18 m (the same as indoor) throughout the 1990s including Atlanta 1996; the FIVB shrank it to 8 m x 16 m ahead of the 2001 season, and beach rally-point scoring only arrived after Sydney 2000 (in force by Athens 2004). Do NOT model an 8x16 court in a 1990s room.

## 2000–2026 — The Global Era (dimpled balls, video challenges, Volleyball World)

**This is the quarter-century when volleyball stopped being a regional sport with a Brazilian dynasty at its centre and became a genuinely global, technologically refereed, commercially packaged spectacle — the ball itself was redesigned twice, the referee got a video screen, the World League became the VNL, and by 2025–26 the trophies were being shared out among Italy, France, Poland, Türkiye and Brazil instead of hoarded by two or three nations.**

### Paleta visual

Two contrasting looks that the room can cross-fade between, because the era itself changed appearance around 2016.

EARLY (2000–2012) — the Brazilian/Athens/Beijing look. Warmer, flatter, more televisual than theatrical. Court colours are pine-hardwood tan or a muted Sydney/Athens teal with brick-red free zones; sponsor boards are static printed vinyl, not LED. Lighting is even, high-CRI metal-halide white with a faint green-cyan cast, no haze, no dark-out — arenas are lit all the way to the back row so the crowd is always visible. Uniforms are looser, heavier polyester in flat block colours: Brazil canary yellow (#F7DD00) over royal blue, Italy azzurro (#0066B3), Russia white/red, USA navy. The MVA200's white-blue-yellow swirl is the brightest object in frame. Typography is condensed grotesque, all-caps, in-camera character generators with hard drop shadows. Materials: painted steel, printed vinyl banners, foam padding, cheap moulded plastic seating, CRT-era scoreboard orange dot-matrix.

LATE (2016–2026) — the Volleyball World look. Dark, saturated, high-contrast, deliberately concert-like. Base is near-black bowl (crowd in shadow) with a single hard white pool of light on the court at broadcast level, haze in the air so the beams read as solid volumes. Court is deep ocean blue (#0B3C8C to #124C9E) inside the lines with a burnt-orange or teal free zone (#D2521E / #0E7C86) and crisp 5 cm white lines. Accent palette from Volleyball World's brand system: electric magenta (#E6007E), cyan (#00A9E0), and a lime-yellow highlight, used on LED ribbon, lower-thirds and challenge graphics. Uniforms are sublimated, matte, body-mapped, with laser-cut vents and gradient prints — Italy's azzurro with a tricolore shoulder wash, France's midnight navy with a red-white chevron, Poland's white-and-crimson, Türkiye's crimson with a white crescent motif. Metals are anodised black and brushed aluminium; surfaces are matte-blacked truss, rubberised camera housings, tempered glass and, everywhere, glowing 10 mm-pitch LED. Typography is a geometric sans in extended and condensed cuts (think a Eurostile/Archivo family), all-caps, tight tracking, with numeric score bugs in a tabular display face. Lighting cues: house-to-black on the introductions, moving-head beams sweeping the crowd, strobe hits on a set point, then a hard snap to flat 1,500+ lux white the moment the whistle goes.

ARCHITECTURE: the room should progress from a daylight-flooded, warm, wide-span 2000s multipurpose gymnasium (exposed white-painted steel trusses, clerestory windows, pale block walls, tan floor) into a sealed black-box modern arena (no daylight, drapes, LED-lined bowl, hanging cube screen) — with the beach volleyball alcove as a bright open-air outlier: raked sand, blue sky, and the Eiffel Tower silhouette on the backdrop.

### Objetos de acervo (13)

#### Mikasa MVA200 — the 8-panel dimpled ball (Beijing 2008, London 2012, Rio 2016)

Sphere, circumference 65–67 cm → diameter 20.7–21.3 cm (model at 21.0 cm / radius 10.5 cm). Weight 260–280 g. Skin is built from EIGHT long, curved, S-shaped 'wave' panels that wrap around the ball in a pinwheel/swirl rather than the traditional 6×3 grid — each panel is roughly a boomerang: ~4–5 cm wide at its waist, flaring to ~7 cm at the ends, meeting its neighbours along smooth continuous seams that spiral over both poles. Seams are recessed grooves ~2 mm deep and ~3 mm wide with a slightly raised lip; there are no visible stitches (the panels are laminated/glued, not sewn). Colourway: white base panels with royal 'Mikasa blue' (approx #1552A0–#1B60AE) and a warm chrome yellow (approx #F2C300–#FFCC00), typically alternating so the ball reads as three broad interlocking swirls of white, blue and yellow when spinning. The signature surface: the entire skin is covered in shallow circular micro-dimples, roughly 1.5–2.5 mm across, ~0.3 mm deep, on a hexagonal-ish packed grid, like a very fine golf ball — they catch specular light as thousands of tiny bright pinpoints, which is the single most recognisable visual cue of the ball. Material is dimpled microfibre synthetic leather over a butyl/latex bladder, satin-matte (not glossy) with faint sheen at the dimple rims. Branding: 'MIKASA' wordmark and 'MVA 200' plus the FIVB roundel silkscreened flat onto two or three panels, ~4–6 cm wide.

*Por que importa:* This is the object that most literally marks the boundary of the era. Wikipedia's ball article states that in 2008 the FIVB adopted a new Mikasa 'with dimples and only eight panels' — the first fundamental redesign of the volleyball's skin in decades, chasing a softer touch and a straighter, less-floating flight. Every serve, dig and spike at three consecutive Olympics (2008, 2012, 2016) happened against this ball.

#### Mikasa V200W — the current 18-panel, double-dimpled FIVB game ball (2019–present)

Same regulation sphere: 65–67 cm circumference, 260–280 g, 0.30–0.325 kg/cm² internal pressure. Unlike the MVA200 it returns to the classic EIGHTEEN-panel architecture: six groups of three near-rectangular panels, each panel roughly 11 × 5 cm with slightly bowed long edges, arranged so the three panels of a group run parallel and each group sits at 90° to its neighbours (the traditional 'volleyball' pattern). Seams are laminated grooves ~2 mm wide, no stitching. Surface is 'double dimpled' — a coarser pattern of shallow dish dimples ~3 mm across with a second, finer micro-texture inside them, giving a subtly quilted look under arena light; cover is Mikasa's 'Super Composite Cover', a microfibre + PU laminate co-developed with Kuraray, with nylon-wound carcass and a Nano Balloon Silica anti-sweat treatment (reads as a slightly chalky, low-gloss finish). Standard FIVB colourway: white panels with one group-band in royal blue (~#1B4F9C) and one in yellow (~#FFD100), producing three broad colour zones. A red/white/navy variant exists for the USA market. Event-specific editions are printed for each World Championship (e.g. the Philippines 2025 and Thailand 2025 editions). Branding: 'MIKASA', 'V200W', 'FIVB APPROVED' and the event logo screen-printed on the white panels.

*Por que importa:* The ball currently in every FIVB rally. Mikasa's own materials say it debuted at the 2019 FIVB World Cup; it is the ball of Tokyo 2020, Paris 2024, the 2025 World Championships and the 2026 VNL. Placing it beside the MVA200 in a display case tells the whole 8-panel → 18-panel story in one glance.

#### The match net, antennae and posts assembly

Net panel: exactly 1.00 m deep, 9.50–10.00 m long, woven from 10 cm square BLACK mesh (cord ~2–3 mm). Along the top, a 7 cm wide horizontal band of two-fold WHITE canvas sewn the full length, with a flexible steel cable threaded through it; along the bottom, a similar 5 cm white band with a rope through it. Two vertical WHITE side bands, 5 cm wide × 1 m tall, are fastened to the net directly above each sideline. ANTENNAE: two flexible fibreglass rods, 1.80 m long and only 10 mm in diameter, clipped to the outer edge of each side band; exactly 80 cm protrudes above the net and that protruding section is banded in alternating 10 cm stripes, conventionally red and white (8 stripes). POSTS: round, smooth, 2.55 m tall, set 0.50–1.00 m outside the sidelines (exactly 1.00 m for FIVB world competitions), typically 8–10 cm diameter anodised aluminium or steel, sleeved to ~2 m height in a padded foam collar (usually branded, ~40–60 cm circumference) and guyless (floor-socket mounted). Net top edge sits at 2.43 m for men, 2.24 m for women. Modern top bands carry sponsor/competition printing in flat vinyl.

*Por que importa:* Everything in volleyball is measured against this plane. The 19 cm difference between the men's and women's net heights, the thin red-and-white antennae defining the legal crossing space, and the black mesh chosen so broadcast cameras can see through it are the invisible design decisions that make the modern televised game legible.

#### The Challenge / video review station: coach's tablet, referee monitor and giant-screen graphic

Three linked props. (1) COACH'S TABLET: a ~10-inch tablet in a rugged black rubber bumper case, tethered on a short coiled cable at the bench end of the scorer's table; its screen shows one large red-orange 'CHALLENGE' button; pressing it fires a horn and stops play, then the screen repopulates with a grid of 4–6 secondary buttons naming the suspected fault (BALL IN/OUT, TOUCH, NET FAULT, ANTENNA, FOOT FAULT, POSITIONAL). (2) REFEREE REVIEW MONITOR: a 22–27 inch matte-black monitor on a short adjustable arm at the scorer's table, angled up, hooded against glare, showing multi-angle synchronised replay windows and a Hawk-Eye ball-trace reconstruction — a wireframe court, a coloured parabola for the ball's path, and an oval 'skid mark' shown either kissing the 5 cm white line (IN) or clear of it (OUT), with a millimetre gap callout. (3) GIANT-SCREEN GRAPHIC: a full-width LED graphic in team colours, typically a dark panel with a bold 'CHALLENGE' lockup, the requesting team's flag, the fault type, an animated countdown, then a slam-cut to the verdict — a green tick 'CHALLENGE SUCCESSFUL' or a red cross 'CHALLENGE UNSUCCESSFUL'. Around the arena, 12 high-resolution Hawk-Eye camera pods (Rio 2016 count): white or black cylindrical housings ~30 × 15 cm on ball-head mounts clamped to catwalks, gantries and the ends of the scaffolding truss above the endlines and sidelines.

*Por que importa:* The Challenge System first appeared at an FIVB event at the 2012 Club World Championships and reached the Olympics at Rio 2016 with 12 Hawk-Eye cameras. Teams may request review twice per set for in/out, block touches, net faults, antenna contact and foot faults. The arena screen is the point: the crowd sees the review, so the referee's authority becomes a shared, theatrical, verifiable moment — the single biggest cultural change to officiating in the sport's history.

#### Modern indoor match uniform (outfield player)

Fully sublimated polyester/elastane knit, 140–160 gsm, matte with laser-cut ventilation zones under the arms and along the flanks. MEN'S: fitted short-sleeve crew or shallow V-neck jersey, hem sitting at the hip, sleeves cut just above the mid-bicep; shorts 15–18 cm inseam, elasticated waist with internal drawcord. WOMEN'S: same jersey block in a narrower cut, often with a racer-shaped sleeve, worn over compression shorts of 5–8 cm inseam (spandex-fit) or the same 15 cm short depending on federation. NUMBERS: heat-transfer or sublimated, restricted to 1–20, in a colour contrasting the jersey; minimum 15 cm tall centred on the chest and minimum 20 cm tall centred on the back. CAPTAIN: a horizontal bar approximately 2 cm tall × at least 8 cm wide, centred immediately under the chest number. Country name or crest above the chest number, manufacturer mark on the right chest, FIVB/competition patch on a sleeve. Two full kit sets per team, one light and one dark, so the two teams never clash. Accessories: hard-shell or gel knee pads (black, 18–22 cm sleeve, EVA foam pad ~10 mm thick over the patella), low-cut gum-soled indoor court shoes in saturated brand colours with visible cushioning pods, ankle braces, occasional finger/thumb taping, and a numbered warm-up top.

*Por que importa:* The 2000s–2020s uniform is the moment volleyball kit becomes broadcast design: sublimation lets federations print gradients, national flags and sponsor blocks straight into the fabric, and the FIVB's number-size and captain-stripe rules exist purely so a television viewer can identify a player mid-jump.

#### The libero's contrasting jersey

Identical cut to the team jersey but in a DIFFERENT DOMINANT COLOUR that clearly contrasts with the rest of the team — the rule is explicit that it must be the dominant colour, not a trim accent. In practice this reads as, e.g., a lime-yellow or white libero shirt against a navy team, or crimson against white. Same number rules apply (1–20, 15 cm chest / 20 cm back). Modern teams carry two liberos: FIVB senior competitions with more than 12 players on the roster REQUIRE two, though only one may be on court at a time. The libero silhouette on the model should be shorter and squatter than the hitters, in a low athletic stance, with heavier, more built-up knee pads (often full wrap-around with additional hip padding) and frequently long sleeves or forearm sleeves for floor dives.

*Por que importa:* The libero was introduced by the FIVB in 1998, and this era is when the role became a star position rather than an oddity — Brazil's Sérgio 'Serginho' Santos and Italy's Monica De Gennaro, who was named MVP of the entire 2025 women's VNL as a libero. The bright odd-coloured shirt is the most instantly readable rule change on any volleyball court and a perfect museum exhibit.

#### Gerflor Taraflex portable competition court

A snap-together vinyl sports floor laid over the arena's concrete or ice slab. Standard elite spec is a 9 mm 'Taraflex Performance' three-colour system covering roughly 693 m². Geometry: the playing court is exactly 18.00 × 9.00 m, split by the centre line, with the attack line 3.00 m from the centre line on each side; all lines are 5 cm wide and white, painted or inlaid so there is no lip. Around it the free zone — minimum 5 m from the sidelines and, in FIVB world competitions, 6.5 m behind the end lines — is laid in a contrasting colour, and the free playing space above must be at least 12.5 m clear. Classic FIVB palette: a saturated royal/ocean blue inside the court with a burnt orange or teal free zone (or the inverse), a hairline white perimeter, and huge flat-printed sponsor/competition logos in the free zone corners and behind the endlines. Surface finish is a low-gloss embossed grain with a fine pebble texture and a polyurethane top layer; underfoot it is a shock-absorbing foam-backed vinyl, giving the characteristic dull thud and slight give. Panels arrive on rolls and join with taped seams that are visible as faint 1–2 mm lines running the length of the hall. Gerflor's Taraflex has been the official volleyball surface at eleven consecutive Olympic Games from Montreal 1976 through Tokyo 2020(21), and for the 2026 VNL Finals Volleyball World and Gerflor unveiled the competition's first ever bespoke Finals court design.

*Por que importa:* The two-tone blue-and-orange portable court is the visual signature of televised volleyball and the thing that makes an anonymous multipurpose arena instantly read as 'volleyball'. It is also a genuine artefact — the same manufacturer's flooring has been rolled out for half a century of Olympic finals.

#### The first referee's stand

A freestanding steel or aluminium platform bolted beside one net post, outside the sideline. Vertical ladder of 4–5 rungs up the back, a small ~50 × 40 cm platform, and a chest-height front rail — set so the first referee's EYES sit roughly 50 cm above the top of the net, looking straight down the plane. The entire structure is sleeved in thick foam padding (typically 5–8 cm) on every face a player could hit, upholstered in branded vinyl in the competition's colours with the sponsor or FIVB/Volleyball World mark printed large. The referee stands in a slate-grey or navy polo and dark trousers, whistle on a lanyard, a small red/yellow card wallet in the pocket, and — in the modern era — a tablet or handset clipped to the rail feeding in/out decisions from the electronic system.

*Por que importa:* It is the sport's pulpit, and in this era it changed function: the man on the box went from sole arbiter to the person who receives an electronic verdict and announces it to a screen-watching crowd.

#### The Olympic gold medal podium tableau, Paris 2024

A three-step podium in the Paris 2024 house style — a low, wide, matte-finish deck in off-white and bronze-gold with the Games emblem inlaid, step heights roughly 45 / 30 / 15 cm and each tread wide enough for a six-player line-up plus staff. Around it, South Paris Arena 1: an exhibition-hall interior converted with black masking drapes, a bowl of dark-blue and purple seating, and a fully lit blue Taraflex court. The medals themselves are discs about 85 mm across and 9.2 mm thick with a hexagonal polished iron insert from the Eiffel Tower at the centre, hung on ribbons. On the top step for the women's event: Italy in blue, Paola Egonu holding the MVP trophy after 22 points in a 3–0 sweep (25–18, 25–20, 25–17) of the USA on 11 August 2024. *Por que importa:* Paris 2024 is the era's closing image: France's back-to-back men's gold (the first since the USA in 1984 and 1988) and Italy's first-ever women's Olympic volleyball title, won 6–0 with a single set dropped and eleven blocks to zero in the final. Two European nations on top, with Brazil, the USA and China off the top step — the exact inverse of 2004.

#### Beach volleyball set-piece: the Eiffel Tower Stadium court, Paris 2024

A temporary stadium on the Champ de Mars with the Eiffel Tower rising directly behind the north stand. The playing court is 16.00 × 8.00 m of levelled, screened sand — no attack line, no centre line, only four 5 cm wide flexible dark-coloured boundary TAPES pegged at the corners, sagging slightly into the sand. Free zone at least 3 m on all sides, and a total sand pit typically 30 × 22 m to at least 40 cm depth. Net 8.5 m long × 1 m deep, 2.43 m for men and 2.24 m for women, with the same 1.80 m red-and-white striped antennae. Ball: 66–68 cm circumference (slightly LARGER than indoor), 260–280 g, inflated to only 0.175–0.225 kg/cm² — visibly softer, in water-resistant textured synthetic leather, usually bright yellow-and-blue or white-and-orange. Two players per side, no substitutions, sets to 21 with a 15-point third set, sides swapped every 7 points (every 5 in the third). Around it: raked sand berms, blue kit-bag benches, sun umbrellas, a DJ booth, and portable LED perimeter boards.

*Por que importa:* Beach volleyball's 8 × 16 m court was only adopted in 2002 after 2001 testing, and the 2012 rule allowing shorts and sleeved tops for women transformed who could play. Paris 2024 — Sweden's Åhman/Hellvig and Brazil's Ana Patrícia/Duda winning under the Eiffel Tower — is the discipline at its most photogenic.

#### LED arena rig: ribbon boards, court-side panels and the show-lighting truss

Three layers to model. (1) PERIMETER LED: a continuous run of ~1 m tall, 10–12 mm pitch LED panels forming a hard rectangle around the free zone, sitting on a low black riser with an angled foam kicker board; content is animated sponsor loops at high brightness, so they wash coloured light onto the court edges. (2) RIBBON/FASCIA LED: a 60–90 cm tall band wrapping the front of the upper tier all the way round the bowl, plus a centre-hung four-sided videoboard cube (or, in smaller halls, two flanking 6 × 4 m screens on ground-support truss behind the endlines) carrying the score bug, replays and the CHALLENGE graphic. (3) LIGHTING RIG: a rectangular grid of black box truss 12–18 m above the floor, carrying rows of white LED sports floodlights aimed to a flat, shadow-free field level (broadcast events run far above the ~300 lux baseline used for ordinary sanctioned play, into the 1,500–2,000 lux range) plus 40–80 moving-head fixtures and strobe bars used only for introductions and timeouts. The signature modern look: house lights dropped to near-black for player entrances, the court a pool of hard white light, saturated beams (Volleyball World's palette runs to magenta, cyan and deep blue) sweeping the crowd, haze in the air making the beams solid, then a hard snap back to flat broadcast white the instant the referee whistles.

*Por que importa:* This is what Volleyball World bought with the CVC partnership announced on 1 February 2021: the sport re-presented as a produced entertainment product. The difference between a 2004 arena and a 2024 arena is not the court — it is the light.

#### Bernardinho's bench: the Brazil technical area, 2001–2017

A six-to-eight-seat run of moulded plastic bench seats in Brazilian yellow behind the sideline, inside the free zone, with a 3–5 m technical-area boundary marked by a dashed line. Props: a stack of A4 stat sheets on a clipboard, an A3 laminated rotation board with magnetic player chips, two laptops running scouting software (Brazil were early and heavy adopters) with a scout operator wearing a headset, a folded towel over each seat back, and a wall of blue-and-yellow water bottles in a wire crate. The coach himself: greying, wiry, in a Brazil polo and dark trousers, permanently on his feet at the edge of the technical area, one arm raised in a rotation call, the other holding a rolled sheet of paper. Behind him a whiteboard and the substitution paddle numbers held by the second referee's assistant.

*Por que importa:* Bernardinho took the Brazil men's job in 2001 and held it until 2017, winning Olympic gold in 2004 and 2016, Olympic silver in 2008 and 2012, World Championships in 2002, 2006 and 2010, World Cups in 2003 and 2007, and eight World League titles between 2001 and 2010 — before returning to the bench at the end of 2023 for the Paris cycle. No coach in team sport has assembled a comparable medal shelf, and the bench is where all of it was directed from.

#### The Volleyball Hall of Fame plaque wall (Holyoke, Massachusetts)

A dark walnut-panelled wall hung with a grid of cast bronze plaques, each roughly 30 × 40 cm, with a raised relief portrait bust on the left two-thirds, a raised name and country line, an induction year, and 6–8 lines of engraved citation text. Patina is dark chocolate brown with polished high points on the nose, brow and shoulders. Mounted 12 cm apart in rows of six, lit from above by small warm 3000 K picture lights on brass arms. The room reads as museum-traditional — deliberately the opposite of the LED arena — with a red carpet runner, a brass rail, and a wall vinyl reading that at the close of the 2025 induction the IVHF had enshrined 185 players, coaches, referees and leaders from 27 countries. Era-relevant plaques to feature: Giba and Hugh McCutcheon (2018), José Roberto Guimarães (2024), Ivan Miljković, Francesca Piccinini, Laura Ludwig and Kent Steffes (2025), and the incoming 2026 class of Ary Graça, April Ross, Fabiana Alvim, Yoel Despaigne, Ekaterina Gamova, Alison Cerutti, Laurent Tillie, Denis Le Breuilly and Takashi Shimoyama.

*Por que importa:* It is literally the sport's museum, in the town where volleyball was invented, and it gives the room a canonical, checkable list of who this era's greats are — a natural in-game collectible or plaque-reading mechanic.
